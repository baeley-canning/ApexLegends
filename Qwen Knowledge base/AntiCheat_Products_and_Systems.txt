KNOWLEDGE BASE: ANTI-CHEAT PRODUCTS AND SYSTEMS

Purpose:
Educational reference describing the major commercial and first-party
game anti-cheat systems by name: who makes each one, what architectural
class it belongs to, which trust anchor it leans on, and what its public
history and trade-offs are.

This document is reference knowledge. It describes systems from a
defensive and engineering perspective, using only publicly documented
facts and vendors' own public statements. It deliberately contains no
recipes for defeating, evading, or bypassing any specific product, and
no operational detail beyond what the vendors themselves publish.

Because these products change frequently and publish only part of their
design, treat every statement here as a publicly documented CLAIM, not
as a verified internal fact. Where something is uncertain it is marked.

Related documents: ANTI-CHEAT ARCHITECTURE, CHEATING IN GAMES - HISTORY,
TAXONOMY, AND DETECTION, ADVERSARIAL AI AND CHEAT DETECTION, ADAPTIVE
CHEATING AND EVOLVING SYSTEMS, HOW ANTI-CHEATS WORK - VANGUARD, BATTLTEYE,
EAC, AND JAVELIN, CLIENT INTEGRITY, ANTI-TAMPER, AND ATTESTATION,
ANTI-CHEAT THREAT MODELING, and the numbered technical series.

---

1. WHY A PRODUCT SURVEY IS USEFUL
The concepts in ANTI-CHEAT ARCHITECTURE are realised, in practice, by a
small number of well-known products.
Knowing the products helps because:
* each is a worked example of the same layered design.
* each makes a different bet about the trust anchor.
* the bets recur: what one product does, others copy.
* shared products define the de-facto baseline players must satisfy.
Reading them together turns an abstract stack into concrete trade-offs.

---

2. A LENS FOR CLASSIFYING ANY ANTI-CHEAT
Any product, past or present, can be placed on a handful of axes:
* WHERE IT RUNS - server-side only, user-mode client, kernel-mode
  driver, or a combination.
* WHEN IT LOADS - on demand with the game, or at boot and persistently.
* ITS TRUST ANCHOR - what it ultimately trusts: its own client, the OS
  kernel, or hardware/boot measurements a cheater cannot easily forge.
* HOW IT DECIDES - signatures/blacklists, behavioural heuristics,
  machine learning, server-side validation, or a mix.
* HOW IT ENFORCES - delayed ban waves, instant kicks, global account or
  hardware bans, or in-match mitigation.
Most confusion about "which anti-cheat is strongest" dissolves once the
products are compared on these axes rather than by reputation.

---

3. RIOT VANGUARD
Vendor: Riot Games. Used by: VALORANT, and described for other Riot
titles.
Publicly documented properties:
* a user-mode component plus a kernel-mode driver.
* the driver loads at boot and stays resident, rather than only while
  the game runs.
* on Windows 11 (and later enforced more widely) VALORANT requires
  TPM 2.0 and Secure Boot, and Riot publishes error codes for missing
  Secure Boot/TPM, Virtualization-Based Security, and exploit
  protection (for example VAN 9001, 9003, 9005, 9002).
* it maintains and updates a blocklist of known-vulnerable drivers.
* Windows-only for the kernel driver.
Trade-off it illustrates: strong kernel plus boot-time trust buys
visibility user mode cannot get, at the cost of persistent privilege
and ongoing controversy over living on every machine at boot.

---

4. BATTLTEYE
Vendor: BattlEye Innovations (Germany), independent since 2004,
originally for the Battlefield series.
Publicly documented properties:
* proactive kernel-based protection combined with detection.
* dynamic, on-the-fly scanning whose targets are chosen by the backend,
  so fixed client files cannot simply be profiled once and ignored.
* a server component that keeps control of the server and can kick
  clients, plus admin interfaces.
* a global ban system tied to account identities (for example
  SteamID-based), claimed by the vendor to rival or exceed the reach of
  VAC bans.
Used by: PUBG, Rainbow Six Siege, the Arma and DayZ lineage, and
others.
Trade-off it illustrates: a mostly proactive kernel defender with a
strong server side and consolidated global bans.

---

5. EASY ANTI-CHEAT (EAC)
Vendor: Epic Games (via the acquisition of Kamu), now part of Epic
Online Services.
Publicly documented properties:
* a modular, layered system with tiered service levels.
* a kernel-mode component for higher-risk competitive titles, with the
  option of lighter configurations.
* cross-platform support including Windows, macOS, Linux and Steam
  Deck, and compatibility with console crossplay.
* tight integration with Epic Online Services (accounts, matchmaking).
Used by: a very large share of PC multiplayer, including Rust, Apex
Legends, Fortnite and many others.
Trade-off it illustrates: standardisation and scale - one vendor,
integrated with a platform, serving many titles across many operating
systems.

---

6. VALVE ANTI-CHEAT (VAC)
Vendor: Valve, first shipped with Counter-Strike in 2002.
Publicly documented properties:
* primarily an automated detection and banning system rather than a
  heavyweight always-on kernel driver.
* bans are issued in delayed "waves" rather than instantly, so that a
  single detection can flag many users of the same cheat before its
  author learns it is detected.
* detection combines known-cheat signatures with behavioural methods;
  Valve has described a machine-learning component (VACnet), a
  community review system (Overwatch in Counter-Strike), and an account
  trust score (Trust Factor).
* bans are typically permanent and non-negotiable.
Used by: Counter-Strike 2, Team Fortress 2, Dota 2 and other
Valve/Steam titles.
Trade-off it illustrates: delayed enforcement as a deliberate
counter-forensics tactic, and community/human review as a complement to
automation.

---

7. FAIRFIGHT
Vendor: GameBlocks, widely used by EA and Ubisoft titles in the 2010s.
Publicly documented properties:
* server-side and statistical/behavioural: it reasons about gameplay
  and account data rather than scanning the client.
* no client component is required for its core function.
* commonly deployed alongside a client anti-cheat (for example with
  BattlEye in Rainbow Six Siege, or alongside other scanners in
  Battlefield titles).
Trade-off it illustrates: a pure server-side approach has no client
footprint to tamper with, but sees only what the server can observe and
is therefore blind to purely local activity.

---

8. BLIZZARD WARDEN
Vendor: Blizzard Entertainment.
Publicly documented properties:
* a client-side scanning system, historically associated with World of
  Warcraft, Overwatch and other Blizzard titles.
* it inspects process and module state and looks for known
  modifications, and has been described as combining client scans with
  server-side analysis.
Trade-off it illustrates: a long-lived first-party scanner, and how a
publisher can run detection in-house rather than buying it.

---

9. PUNKBUSTER (LEGACY)
Vendor: Even Balance, first released in 2000.
Publicly documented properties:
* a client that scans memory for known cheat signatures against a
  database, plus periodic status reports from client to server.
* server options to request file hashes of the game installation and to
  capture player screenshots for review.
* a "heartbeat" streaming model, where findings could be shared to
  community ban lists.
Used by: many 2000s shooters (the early Call of Duty and Battlefield
games, Return to Castle Wolfenstein, and others).
Status: largely retired from current titles, but historically
influential.

---

10. NPROTECT GAMEGUARD (LEGACY)
Vendor: INCA Internet (South Korea).
Publicly documented properties:
* often described as rootkit-like: it hides its own and the game's
  process, monitors memory, blocks certain API and DirectX calls, and
  blocks programs the vendor defines as cheats.
* long used across Korean and Asian online games (the Lineage and
  MapleStory lineages among many).
* has had documented security problems, including an old
  privilege-escalation issue in a driver component.
Status: classified as legacy/inactive in current references.

---

11. XIGNCODE3
Vendor: Wellbia (South Korea).
Publicly documented properties:
* a client anti-cheat with kernel-level components, deployed widely in
  Asian and global releases (for example ArcheAge and some music games).
* like its peers, it runs alongside the game process and reports to a
  backend.
Trade-off it illustrates: several regional vendors offer the same
kernel-plus-backend pattern, which is why the pattern - not any one
brand - is the thing worth understanding.

---

12. ANTI-CHEAT EXPERT (TENCENT ACE, FORMERLY TP)
Vendor: Tencent. First appeared around 2005 as an anti-bot, rebranded
as Anti-Cheat Expert in 2021.
Publicly documented properties:
* a kernel-level anti-cheat for PC and mobile, used across Tencent's
  own large titles and licensed to others (for example Honor of Kings,
  Delta Force, and many cross-platform releases).
* has drawn the same kernel-access criticism as other kernel products,
  and has been reported to block desktop Linux/Proton in some titles
  while permitting handheld variants.
Trade-off it illustrates: at platform scale, the same kernel model is
applied to hundreds of millions of players, magnifying both its reach
and its privacy/security concerns.

---

13. RICOCHET (CALL OF DUTY)
Vendor: Activision.
Publicly documented properties:
* combines a kernel-level driver on PC with server-side mitigations.
* beyond banning, it has publicly described in-match measures such as
  making a detected cheater unable to see or affect legitimate players.
Trade-off it illustrates: enforcement can be a spectrum - from bans to
live mitigation that reduces harm before a ban lands - and server-side
authority is what makes some mitigations possible at all.

---

14. JAVELIN AND EA ANTICHEAT
Vendor: Electronic Arts.
Publicly documented properties:
* EA AntiCheat (EAAC) is EA's in-house kernel-level anti-cheat,
  introduced with its 2022 titles.
* Javelin is the anti-cheat EA introduced for Battlefield 6 (2025); EA
  publicly stated it blocked hundreds of thousands of cheat attempts
  around launch.
* EA's earlier PC titles leaned on a mix of client scanners (such as
  PunkBuster, then EAC) and the server-side FairFight system.
Trade-off it illustrates: large publishers increasingly build and own
their anti-cheat, so detection is not outsourced and can be tuned to
one engine and one title.

---

15. DENUVO ANTI-CHEAT
Vendor: Denuvo Software Solutions, now owned by Irdeto - the same
company known for the Denuvo Anti-Tamper DRM.
Publicly documented properties:
* a kernel-level anti-cheat offering.
* because the vendor also sells copy-protection, the anti-cheat and the
  DRM are often discussed together, and both have attracted strong
  player criticism.
Trade-off it illustrates: anti-cheat can be a product line adjacent to
copy-protection, and reputation from one can colour the other.

---

16. THIRD-PARTY COMPETITIVE CLIENTS (FACEIT, ESEA)
Not game-wide anti-cheat, but league/competition clients:
* FACEIT's client publicly documents kernel-level components and
  hardware/account requirements.
* the ESEA client is a long-standing example; it was at the centre of a
  2013 incident in which the installed client was found to be mining
  cryptocurrency, a cautionary tale about trust in a mandatory client.
Trade-off they illustrate: competition-grade integrity can be bought
separately from the game, and a privileged client is itself a security
liability if its operator misuses it.

---

17. PLATFORM AND FIRST-PARTY SCANNERS
Some vendors ship their own, less publicly detailed systems:
* Roblox acquired Byfron (maker of the Hyperion anti-cheat) and uses it
  on the Windows client.
* AhnLab's HackShield and other regional products have protected many
  Asian titles, sometimes replaced over time by EAC or XIGNCODE3.
* console platforms enforce integrity largely at the platform level
  rather than through per-game clients.
The lesson: the kernel-plus-backend pattern is industry-wide, and the
brand matters far less than the class of system.

---

18. THE KERNEL-MODE DEFAULT
Across the current leaders - Vanguard, BattlEye, EAC, ACE, Ricochet,
EAAC/Javelin, Denuvo Anti-Cheat - the shared design decision is a
kernel-mode component.
The reason is the trust argument from ANTI-CHEAT ARCHITECTURE: a
user-mode observer can be hidden from or manipulated by something
underneath it, so the observer is pushed below the observed.
This is also why the debate about these products is really a debate
about kernel privilege, not about any single vendor.

---

19. BOOT-START AND ALWAYS-ON
Vanguard popularised loading its driver at boot and leaving it
resident.
Consequences worth noting:
* maximum visibility into early-boot and low-level activity.
* the protection is present even when the game is not running.
* the cost is constant privilege and constant attention from privacy
  and security researchers.
Not every kernel product does this; the boot-start choice is a
deliberate point on the trade-off curve.

---

20. HARDWARE-BACKED REQUIREMENTS AND ATTESTATION
The newest layer is requiring the platform itself to be in a known-good
state before play is allowed.
Publicly visible examples:
* TPM 2.0 and Secure Boot requirements (Vanguard).
* Virtualization-Based Security and exploit-protection checks.
* vendor blocklists of vulnerable drivers, and IOMMU/DMA protection.
This moves the anchor of trust toward hardware and boot measurements -
the part of the machine a cheater running underneath the game cannot
easily forge.

---

21. SERVER-SIDE-ONLY SYSTEMS
Not everything is a client.
FairFight is the clearest example of a system that reasons entirely on
the server.
Its strength is that the defender fully controls the measurement; its
limitation is that purely local advantages it cannot observe remain
invisible.
This is why real deployments increasingly combine a server-side
authority with one or more client-side detectors.

---

22. COMPARISON OVERVIEW
A compact, approximate map (details change; publicly documented
classifications only):
Riot Vanguard    - kernel + user client, boot-start, TPM/Secure Boot
BattlEye         - kernel + server, proactive, global account bans
Easy Anti-Cheat  - modular kernel/user, part of Epic Online Services
Valve Anti-Cheat - automated detection, delayed ban waves, ML + review
FairFight        - server-side behavioural only
Warden           - first-party client scanner
PunkBuster       - legacy client scanner + server
GameGuard        - legacy client, rootkit-like, Korean market
XIGNCODE3        - client + kernel, Asian market
Tencent ACE      - kernel, platform scale, PC and mobile
Ricochet         - kernel driver + server-side mitigation
EAAC / Javelin   - in-house kernel, EA titles
Denuvo Anti-Cheat- kernel, from the DRM vendor
The columns that matter: where it runs, when it loads, and what it
ultimately trusts.

---

23. HOW TO EVALUATE A PRODUCT YOU ENCOUNTER
Ask the five lens questions from section 2, in order:
* Does it run on the server, the client, or the kernel - or several?
* Does it load with the game, or at boot and persist?
* What does it ultimately trust: its own process, the OS kernel, or
  hardware/boot state?
* How does it decide: signatures, behaviour, ML, server validation?
* How does it enforce: ban waves, kicks, hardware bans, live
  mitigation?
Then ask the trust question that underlies all of them: what would
have to be true about the machine for this product's evidence to be
sound, and who controls that?
Notice too what the vendor does NOT publish: silence about internals
is itself the norm, and is a security choice, not an accident.

---

24. COMMON MISREADINGS
* "Kernel anti-cheat is unbeatable." No - it raises the cost and moves
  the observation point; it is not absolute.
* "Server-side means no cheating." Server-side authority removes some
  classes of advantage but is blind to others.
* "A kernel driver is automatically malware." It is a trust decision
  and a privilege level, not a verdict; whether it is acceptable
  depends on the operator and the guarantees given.
* "Brand X is the real anti-cheat." Products come and go; the classes,
  layers and trade-offs persist.
* "Detected equals banned." All the serious systems separate detection
  from enforcement, often by a wide margin in time.

---

25. SUMMARY
The anti-cheat market is a small number of archetypes repeated under
many names.
Almost every current product is some combination of: a kernel or
user-mode client, a server-side authority, a detection engine
(signature, behavioural, or ML), and an enforcement system (delayed
bans, hardware/account bans, or live mitigation) - with a growing
dependence on hardware and boot trust.
Understanding the archetype lets you reason about a product you have
never seen, and about the next one before it ships.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
The name on the anti-cheat is far less important than the class of
system underneath it. Every serious product answers the same three
questions - where does the observer run, when does it load, and what
does it ultimately trust - and the whole field is converging on the
same answers: push the observer below the observed (kernel mode),
establish trust before the game starts (boot and hardware attestation),
and never rely on the client alone (keep a server-side authority). Read
a product by its archetype and its trust anchor, not by its marketing,
and remember that detection and enforcement are deliberately separate
stages.
