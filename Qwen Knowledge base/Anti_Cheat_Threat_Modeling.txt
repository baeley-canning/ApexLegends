KNOWLEDGE BASE: ANTI-CHEAT THREAT MODELING

Purpose:
Provide a repeatable method for reasoning about any anti-cheat system:
its assets, adversaries, trust boundaries, attack surfaces, and
assumptions. It is a capstone that ties together ANTI-CHEAT ARCHITECTURE,
SERVER-AUTHORITATIVE ARCHITECTURE AND TRUST BOUNDARIES, and HOW
ANTI-CHEATS WORK - VANGUARD, BATTLTEYE, EAC, AND JAVELIN.

This document is reference knowledge. It describes a defensive analysis
method, using publicly documented practice. It contains no instructions
for attacking any system.

Threat models depend on current designs and disclosures, so treat each
statement as a documented CLAIM. Details are given "as of October 2026".

Related documents: ANTI-CHEAT ARCHITECTURE, SERVER-AUTHORITATIVE
ARCHITECTURE AND TRUST BOUNDARIES, ADAPTIVE CHEATING, RED TEAMING, AND
EVOLVING ANTI-CHEAT SYSTEMS, HARDWARE, DMA, AND BELOW-KERNEL THREATS, and
the numbered technical series.

---

1. WHY A METHOD, NOT A CHECKLIST
A checklist catches known problems; a method catches new ones.
Anti-cheat changes constantly, so a static list ages badly.
A threat model is a way of asking the right questions of any design.
The goal is to reason, not to memorise.

---

2. THE ASSETS
Start by naming what is being protected.
The assets are the integrity of the game logic, the fairness of the
match, the value of the account, and the trust of the players.
Different assets call for different defences.
You cannot protect what you have not named.

---

3. THE ADVERSARIES
Name who might attack and with what motivation.
They range from curious amateurs to organised sellers with business
models.
Each adversary has different resources and persistence.
Modelling the strongest plausible adversary sets the bar.

---

4. THE TRUST BOUNDARIES
Draw every line where trust changes hands.
Client to server, user mode to kernel, kernel to hardware, player to
provider.
Each boundary is where an assumption can be tested.
Trust boundaries are where most attacks live.

---

5. THE ATTACK SURFACES
At each boundary, list what an attacker could influence.
Input, memory, network timing, device identity, and boot state are common
surfaces.
Map each surface to the cheat classes that exploit it.
Surfaces are the places where the model meets reality.

---

6. THE ASSUMPTIONS AND WHERE THEY BREAK
Every defence rests on assumptions.
Name them explicitly: that hardware is honest, that the client is intact,
that the server validates.
Then ask what happens if each is false.
An unexamined assumption is the most common hidden weakness.

---

7. THE FIVE QUESTIONS AS A LENS
Ask where the observer runs, when it loads, what it trusts, how it
decides, and how it enforces.
These five answers place any product on the timing, trust, and ownership
axes.
The questions convert a design into a set of testable claims.
They are the practical core of this method.

---

8. SYSTEMATIC PROMPTS
A few prompts make the analysis repeatable:
what would have to be true for this defence to hold?
who controls each thing it trusts?
what does it not observe, and could that matter?
what happens when two defences disagree?
Prompts keep the analysis honest and comparable across systems.

---

9. SEVERITY AND LIKELIHOOD
Not every risk deserves equal effort.
Rate each threat by impact and by how easily it can be realised.
Spend defences where severity and likelihood are both high.
Prioritisation is what turns a model into decisions.

---

10. DEFENCE IN DEPTH
Because single defences fail, layer independent ones.
Each layer should catch what the layer beneath it misses.
Layers should differ in kind, not just in amount.
Independence is what makes depth real rather than nominal.

---

11. VALIDATING WITH RED TEAMING
A model is a hypothesis until it is tested.
Red teaming tries to falsify it under controlled, ethical conditions.
See ADAPTIVE CHEATING, RED TEAMING, AND EVOLVING ANTI-CHEAT SYSTEMS for
the method.
A model that survives testing is worth more than one that only sounds
complete.

---

12. KEEPING THE MODEL CURRENT
Designs, adversaries, and disclosures all change.
A model is a living document, not a one-time artefact.
Review it when a new cheat class appears or a defence changes.
Staleness is its own vulnerability.

---

13. SUMMARY
A threat model is a method for asking the right questions, not a
checklist.
Name the assets, the adversaries, the trust boundaries, the surfaces, and
the assumptions.
The five questions place any product on the timing, trust, and ownership
axes.
Prioritise by severity and likelihood, and layer independent defences.
Validate by red teaming and keep the model current.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
Threat modelling is the discipline of finding the assumption a defence
cannot live without. Name the assets, the adversaries, and every trust
boundary; then ask what would have to be true for the protection to hold,
and who controls that. A model you test and update beats a checklist you
memorise, because the field changes faster than any list.
