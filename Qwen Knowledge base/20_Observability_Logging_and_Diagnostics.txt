KNOWLEDGE BASE: OBSERVABILITY, LOGGING, AND DIAGNOSTICS

Purpose:
Educational reference for making a running system understandable:
recording what it did, measuring how it behaves, and capturing evidence
that turns a vague problem into a specific, testable question.

---

1. WHY OBSERVABILITY
A system that cannot be observed cannot be reasoned about.
Observability is the ability to ask new questions of a running system
without changing it.
It is the difference between guessing and knowing.

---

2. THE THREE SIGNALS
Logs record discrete events.
Metrics record numeric behaviour over time.
Traces record the path and timing of a request or frame.
Together they cover what happened, how much, and where time went.

---

3. STRUCTURED LOGGING
Structured logs are key-value records, not free text.
They can be filtered, searched, and aggregated.
A message plus fields such as stage, duration, and outcome is far more
useful than a sentence.

---

4. LOG LEVELS
Levels separate routine detail from problems.
Debug for development, info for significant events, warning for
recoverable trouble, error for failures.
Consistent use of levels keeps signal visible above noise.

---

5. WHAT TO LOG AND WHAT NOT
Log decisions, boundaries, and failures with enough context to
reconstruct them.
Do not log secrets, keys, or bulk payloads.
Excessive logging hides the important entries and costs storage.

---

6. CORRELATION IDENTIFIERS
A shared identifier ties related records together across stages.
It lets a single frame or request be followed end to end.
Without it, events are disconnected fragments.

---

7. METRICS
Metrics are numeric measurements collected over time.
They answer "how much," "how often," and "how variable."
They are cheap to record and easy to aggregate.

---

8. COUNTERS, GAUGES, AND HISTOGRAMS
Counters only increase and record totals.
Gauges record a current value.
Histograms record a distribution, which is what latency needs.

---

9. PERCENTILES
The p95 and p99 capture the tail that averages hide.
Users experience the tail, not the mean.
Report percentiles for anything time-related.

---

10. TRACING
A trace follows one unit of work through all its stages.
It shows where time is spent and where delays accumulate.
It turns an end-to-end number into an explainable breakdown.

---

11. SPANS AND STAGES
Each stage of work is a span with a start, duration, and parent.
Nested spans reveal the structure and the dominant stage.

---

12. REPRODUCIBLE-EVIDENCE CAPTURE
When something seems wrong, capture the exact inputs, outputs, and
timings that demonstrate it.
A minimal, repeatable case is worth more than a description.
Evidence should let someone else reproduce the observation.

---

13. PROBES AND MINIMAL EXPERIMENTS
A probe is a small, focused measurement built to answer one question.
Keep probes small, isolated, and temporary, and clean them up.
They convert a suspicion into a reading.

---

14. GROUND TRUTH COMPARISON
Compare measured values against a known reference, not against
expectation.
Requested size versus actual size, expected coordinate versus observed
coordinate, and similar pairs localise errors precisely.

---

15. DIAGNOSING WITH EVIDENCE
State the observation before the explanation.
The observation is a fact; the explanation is a hypothesis.
Separating them prevents fixing the wrong thing.

---

16. DESIRED VERSUS OBSERVED
The clearest diagnosis names what was expected and what occurred.
This frames the gap and points to where they diverge.

---

17. ISOLATION AND BISECTION
Change one variable at a time, or bisect the system, to find where
behaviour changes.
Eliminating possibilities is as valuable as confirming them.

---

18. HEALTH AND READINESS
A health check answers whether the system is functioning; readiness
answers whether it can serve now.
They are different questions and often need different probes.

---

19. ALERTING AND NOISE
An alert should correspond to something worth acting on.
Alert fatigue from noisy thresholds makes real problems invisible.
Tune alerts to the cost of the condition, not to every fluctuation.

---

20. RETENTION AND COST
Storing everything forever is neither affordable nor useful.
Retain detail long enough to investigate, and summarise the rest.

---

21. DASHBOARDS
A dashboard shows the few signals that matter at a glance.
Its purpose is orientation, not completeness.
The best panels answer the questions asked most often.

---

22. SHARING DIAGNOSTICS SAFELY
Logs and traces often leave the machine.
Remove secrets and personal data before sharing.
Treat any captured evidence as if it could be published.

---

23. SUMMARY
Observability turns a running system into an interrogable one.
Structured logs, metrics, and traces answer different questions;
percentiles and traces expose where time really goes.
Turning a problem into reproducible evidence is the core diagnostic
skill.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
You cannot fix what you cannot see, and you cannot see what you do not
record. Capture structured events, measure distributions and percentiles,
and turn every problem into a minimal, reproducible piece of evidence.
State observations before explanations, and compare against ground truth
rather than expectation.
