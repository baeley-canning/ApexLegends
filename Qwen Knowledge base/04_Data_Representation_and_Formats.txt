KNOWLEDGE BASE: DATA REPRESENTATION AND FORMATS

Purpose:
Educational reference for how information is represented as bits and
bytes: how numbers, text, images, and coordinates are encoded, and why
representation choices cause bugs that look like logic errors.

---

1. EVERYTHING IS BITS
All data is a sequence of bits, grouped into bytes.
Meaning comes from interpretation: the same bytes can be a number, text,
a colour, or an instruction depending on how they are read.
Most "wrong value" bugs are really "wrong interpretation" bugs.

---

2. INTEGER REPRESENTATION
Integers are exact within their range but wrap or overflow outside it.
Signed and unsigned interpretations of the same bits differ.
Fixed-width types silently truncate, which is a recurring source of
errors in coordinates, sizes, and counters.

---

3. FLOATING-POINT
Floating-point represents a wide range of magnitudes with limited
precision.
Most decimal fractions cannot be stored exactly, so equality tests fail
and small errors accumulate.
Compare with tolerances; never assume two independently computed floats
are exactly equal.

---

4. TEXT AND ENCODING
Text is bytes plus an encoding that maps byte sequences to characters.
An encoding mismatch produces garbled text, not a clean error.
Keeping one encoding (for example UTF-8) end to end avoids it.

---

5. IMAGES AS DATA
An image is a grid of pixels, each holding colour in channels.
Channels may be ordered differently (for example RGB versus BGR) and may
include an alpha channel for transparency.
Swapped channels produce plausible-looking but wrong colours, a classic
integration bug.

---

6. COLOUR, RESOLUTION, AND DEPTH
Colour depth is the number of bits per channel; higher depth means finer
gradation.
Resolution is the pixel count; more pixels mean more data and more cost.
Compression may be lossless or lossy, and lossy compression changes the
values themselves.

---

7. PIXEL FORMATS AND STRIDE
A pixel format defines channel order and bits per channel.
Stride is the number of bytes between the start of one row and the next,
which may exceed the visible row width.
Ignoring stride is a common cause of skewed or sheared images.

---

8. COORDINATE CONVENTIONS
Conventions differ: origin at top-left or bottom-left; x then y, or row
then column.
Units may be pixels, degrees, or world units.
Mixing conventions silently displaces everything drawn or measured.

---

9. UNITS AND DIMENSIONS
Every physical quantity carries a unit; adding metres to pixels is
meaningless.
Carrying units through a computation catches errors early.
Dimensionless ratios and angles are the exception, and should be labelled
as such.

---

10. SERIALISATION
Serialisation turns structured data into bytes for storage or transfer,
and deserialisation reverses it.
Both ends must agree on the format, including ordering and types.
Binary and text formats trade compactness against readability.

---

11. BYTE ORDER
Multi-byte values can be stored most-significant first or last
(endianness).
Reading a value in the wrong order produces a scrambled number.
Network and file formats specify their byte order precisely.

---

12. WHY REPRESENTATION ERRORS HIDE
A wrong format often produces plausible output rather than a crash.
Swapped channels, offsets, and stride errors look like design mistakes.
When output is subtly wrong, suspect the representation before the
algorithm.

---

13. SUMMARY
Data is bits plus an interpretation: numbers, text, images, and
coordinates each have their own representation rules.
Precision, channel order, stride, units, and frames are the recurring
sources of error.
Most "wrong value" bugs are representation mismatches, not logic bugs.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
Data is meaningless without its interpretation. The same bytes can be a
number, a colour, or text, and a mismatch rarely crashes - it quietly
produces plausible but wrong output. Before debugging the logic, verify
the representation: precision, channel order, stride, units, and
coordinate frame.
