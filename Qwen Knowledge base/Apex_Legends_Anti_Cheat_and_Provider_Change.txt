KNOWLEDGE BASE: APEX LEGENDS ANTI-CHEAT AND THE PROVIDER CHANGE

Purpose:
Reference note on the anti-cheat protection used by the game Apex
Legends, and on the change of provider announced in September 2026 when
the PC client moved from Easy Anti-Cheat (EAC) to EA Javelin Anticheat.
It explains what was replaced, what replaced it, the reasons EA and
Respawn gave, and how to read such a change in general.

This document is reference knowledge. It describes systems from a
defensive and engineering perspective, using only publicly documented
facts and the vendors' own public statements. It deliberately contains
no recipes for defeating, evading, or bypassing any protection, and no
operational detail beyond what the vendors themselves publish.

Anti-cheat products change frequently and publish only part of their
design, so treat every statement here as a publicly documented CLAIM,
not as a verified internal fact. Details are given "as of October 2026".

Related documents: ANTI-CHEAT ARCHITECTURE, ANTI-CHEAT PRODUCTS AND
SYSTEMS, CHEATING IN GAMES - HISTORY, TAXONOMY, AND DETECTION,
HARDWARE, DMA, AND BELOW-KERNEL THREATS, SERVER-AUTHORITATIVE
ARCHITECTURE AND TRUST BOUNDARIES, HOW ANTI-CHEATS WORK - VANGUARD,
BATTLTEYE, EAC, AND JAVELIN, ESPORTS AND COMPETITIVE INTEGRITY, and
the numbered technical series.

---

1. WHAT THIS DOCUMENT COVERS
Apex Legends is one of the largest competitive shooters, and its
anti-cheat is watched closely because cheating has been a persistent
problem there.
In September 2026 its PC anti-cheat provider changed for the first time
since launch.
This document records what changed, why, and what the change illustrates
about how the industry chooses and swaps anti-cheat systems.

---

2. THE GAME AND WHY ITS ANTI-CHEAT MATTERS
Apex Legends is a free-to-play battle royale hero shooter developed by
Respawn Entertainment and published by Electronic Arts (EA).
It launched on February 4, 2019, and hosts a large ranked scene and the
Apex Legends Global Series (ALGS) esports circuit.
A free, mass-market, high-skill competitive game is an attractive target
for cheating, so its anti-cheat is part of its business, not a side
feature.
Competitive integrity - the belief that results reflect skill - is the
asset the anti-cheat exists to protect.

---

3. WHERE APEX STARTED - EASY ANTI-CHEAT
From launch, the PC client used Easy Anti-Cheat (EAC), a kernel-level
anti-cheat service made by Epic Games (the product originated with the
Finnish company Kamu, which Epic acquired in 2018).
EAC is part of Epic Online Services and is used across a very large
share of PC multiplayer games.
EA's own August 2024 statement described it as "a kernel level
anti-cheat service" and noted that Apex layered additional, partly
in-house protections on top.
Apex also opted into EAC's Linux support, which broadened the platform
reach but, as the community observed, also widened where cheating
software could run.

---

4. THE CHEATING PRESSURE
In an August 22, 2024 anti-cheat update, EA and Respawn said more than
6 million accounts had been banned since launch.
They said the game averaged more than 100,000 bans per month.
They expanded the anti-cheat team for 24/7 coverage and added people in
the Asia-Pacific region, which was described as especially hard hit.
The tone of that post - the admission that they had not "been as
successful as we've needed to be" - is itself evidence that the
incumbent protection was under sustained pressure.

---

5. THE MARCH 2024 ALGS INCIDENT
During the ALGS North America Pro League Split 1 Finals on March 18,
2024, cheats were remotely injected into the clients of professional
players (reported to include TSM's ImperialHal and Genburten) during a
live broadcast.
The activity was attributed to a person using the name "Destroyer2009".
It was widely reported at the time as a remote code execution (RCE)
exploit, although EAC publicly denied that its anti-cheat was the cause
and later reporting disputed the RCE framing.
The finals were postponed.
Whatever the precise mechanism, the episode put competitive integrity
and the limits of the existing protection in front of the whole
audience.

---

6. THE ANNOUNCEMENT - EAC TO EA JAVELIN
On September 24, 2026, EA and Respawn announced that the Apex Legends
PC anti-cheat would switch to EA Javelin Anticheat.
The change took effect on September 29, 2026.
EA thanked the Easy Anti-Cheat team at Epic Games for what it called "a
long and fruitful partnership".
PC players received a game update and were migrated to Javelin
automatically on the next launch.
Console anti-cheat was explicitly left unchanged.

---

7. WHAT EA JAVELIN ANTICHEAT IS
EA Javelin Anticheat is Electronic Arts' own in-house, kernel-mode PC
anti-cheat, part of what EA calls its "defense-in-depth" strategy.
It is a first-party product: developed, owned, and operated by EA
Security rather than licensed from a third party.
EA states it runs at kernel level because detecting external cheats
requires it, not for deeper access for its own sake.
EA states it runs only while a Javelin-protected game is running, and
that it uninstalls itself from a PC once no Javelin-protected games
remain installed.
EA's public claims include a detection accuracy rate above 99% and a
false-positive rate below 1%.

---

8. TIMELINE OF EA JAVELIN
September 2022: EA launches its in-house PC anti-cheat under the name
EA Anti-Cheat (EAAC).
April 24, 2025: EA reintroduces the same line under a new brand, "EA
Javelin Anticheat", describing it as the evolution of its flagship
anti-cheat solution.
Over those years it protected many EA titles, including EA SPORTS FC,
Madden NFL, Battlefield, F1, WRC, Plants vs. Zombies, skate., and
Battlefield 6.
September 10, 2026: an EA progress report cites 20 protected titles,
57.1 million players, and 26.7 million cheat attempts prevented over
the year.
September 29, 2026: Apex Legends becomes one of the protected titles.

---

9. WHAT CHANGED FOR APEX PLAYERS
In practice, the PC client's anti-cheat files and kernel driver changed
from EAC to Javelin after an ordinary game update.
Unlike some earlier EA titles, Apex did not require Secure Boot at the
time of the switch; EA stated plainly that players would not need to
change BIOS settings.
The migration was automatic: update, launch, and play.
Console cheating continues to be addressed by data models and
server-side detection rather than a PC kernel driver.

---

10. THE REASONS EA AND RESPAWN GAVE
The announcement gives four linked reasons.
Greater visibility into emerging threats.
More flexibility to develop and improve detections as cheating evolves.
Bringing anti-cheat engineering, telemetry, and data capabilities
together in-house for more speed and visibility.
Maintaining what they call a high standard of competitive integrity in
the Apex Games.

---

11. THE STRUCTURAL REASONS BENEATH THE STATED ONES
When a publisher replaces a third-party anti-cheat with its own, several
general forces are usually also at work.
Control: first-party code can be updated on the publisher's own
schedule rather than a vendor's release cadence.
Data: the publisher keeps the detection telemetry and can fuse it with
its own account, matchmaking, and business data.
Cost and leverage: a large publisher can amortise one in-house system
across many titles instead of licensing per title.
Roadmap: features such as anti-DMA protection, Secure Boot, and
input-manipulation defence can be built once and reused everywhere.
None of these is stated as a reason in the Apex announcement, but
together they explain why first-party replacement is a recurring
industry pattern.

---

12. WHY A FIRST-PARTY SYSTEM IS ATTRACTIVE TO A PUBLISHER
A first-party anti-cheat can be tuned to the publisher's own games and
threat model rather than a generic, multi-customer baseline.
It lets the publisher set the privacy posture, the boot-time
requirements, and the enforcement policy directly.
It removes a dependency on a partner's or competitor's product and on
that product's roadmap.
It concentrates anti-cheat expertise inside the company that also owns
the accounts, the storefront, and the esports league.
This is the same logic behind Riot Vanguard and Valve Anti-Cheat, both
of which are first-party.

---

13. WHAT DOES NOT CHANGE
The switch is a change of tool, not a change of architecture.
The model stays layered: an observing client, a kernel component, a
server-side authority, behavioural and machine-learning analysis, and
account/device identity.
Enforcement stays separate from detection, with ban waves, appeals, and
delayed action.
Console protection and server-side detection remain in place regardless
of which PC provider is used.

---

14. HOW TO READ ANY PROVIDER CHANGE
Ask what changed: which layer (client, kernel, or server) and which
vendor.
Ask what was kept: usually most of the stack is unchanged.
Ask what drove it: cheating pressure, platform requirements, cost,
control, or a specific incident.
Separate the stated reasons from the structural ones; both are real and
usually both are present.
Then apply the standard questions from ANTI-CHEAT ARCHITECTURE: where
does the observer run, when does it load, and what does it ultimately
trust.
Those answers, not the brand name, decide what the change means.

---

15. SUMMARY
Apex Legends used Easy Anti-Cheat from its 2019 launch and moved its PC
anti-cheat to EA's first-party EA Javelin Anticheat on September 29,
2026, announced on September 24, 2026.
The change followed years of cheating pressure, including the March
2024 ALGS incident, and EA's stated reasons were greater threat
visibility, faster detection development, and in-house control for
competitive integrity.
Javelin is EA's rebranded, in-house, kernel-mode anti-cheat, formerly
called EA Anti-Cheat and launched in 2022.
Console anti-cheat, layered defence, and server-side authority are
unchanged.
Read the change as a swap of tool inside the same architecture, and as
the recurring pattern of publishers replacing third-party anti-cheat
with their own.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
When a game changes anti-cheat providers, the headline is the brand but
the substance is the architecture: find which layer and trust anchor
changed and which stayed the same. Apex Legends moving from Easy
Anti-Cheat to EA Javelin Anticheat is a publisher trading a third-party
kernel anti-cheat for a first-party one to gain visibility, control,
and speed - the same trade Riot and Valve made with Vanguard and VAC.
The durable lesson is not the name but the pattern: cheating pressure
plus the publisher's appetite for control moves anti-cheat in-house,
while the layered, server-authoritative design of the system stays put.
