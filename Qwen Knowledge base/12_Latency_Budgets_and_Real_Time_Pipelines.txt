KNOWLEDGE BASE: LATENCY BUDGETS AND REAL-TIME PIPELINES

Purpose:
Educational reference for reasoning about latency in systems that
observe and react to real-time applications: how to break a total
response time into stages, where queueing and buffering add delay, and
how throughput, latency, and jitter trade against each other.

This document is reference knowledge. It describes mechanisms, their
consequences, and how to reason about failures. It is written from a
defensive and engineering perspective. It is the applied counterpart to
the real-time systems reference, which covers scheduling in general.

---

1. LATENCY VERSUS THROUGHPUT
Two systems can move the same number of frames per second and feel
completely different.
Throughput is how much work completes per unit time.
Latency is how long one unit of work takes from start to finish.
Jitter is how much that delay varies from one unit to the next.
A pipeline tuned for throughput uses large batches and deep buffers; one
tuned for latency uses small buffers and immediate handoff and tolerates
spikes poorly.
Most perception-and-react systems are latency-sensitive rather than
throughput-sensitive, which is why the distinction is the first thing to
establish when analysing one.

---

2. BREAKING DOWN A RESPONSE-TIME BUDGET
A useful habit is to allocate a total latency budget and then divide it.
For a system that senses the world and acts on it, the stages are
roughly:
* sense: acquire the input (a frame, a sample, or an event).
* process: perceive and decide.
* act: emit the output.
* propagate: the output reaches the world (and, if a human reacts, the
  human loop closes too).
The stages are in series, so their latencies add rather than average.
A pipeline that is fast at every stage but buffers between them can
still be slow end to end.

---

3. WHERE THE TIME GOES: QUEUES AND BUFFERS
Delay is usually not computation; it is waiting.
Common sources:
* queue depth: work waiting behind other work.
* double or triple buffering: a frame is held to smooth output.
* batching: inputs collected over an interval before processing.
* synchronisation points: waiting for a producer to finish.
Every buffer that smooths output also adds at least one frame of delay.
The latency-versus-smoothness trade is made one buffer at a time, and
often by default rather than by choice.

---

4. JITTER AND TAIL LATENCY
Average latency hides the cases users actually notice.
Jitter is the spread; tail latency is the worst cases (the 95th and 99th
percentiles).
A pipeline with a good average and a bad tail feels unreliable, because
reaction depends on the worst frame, not the best.
Occupancy and scheduling - how many threads run and how they are
prioritised - often dominate the tail far more than raw average speed.

---

5. MEASURING LATENCY HONESTLY
Counters that measure only internal processing miss the sense and
propagate stages.
Measuring end to end usually requires an external reference: a timestamp
taken where the event enters and another where the output leaves.
Without an external reference it is easy to optimise a stage that was
never the bottleneck, because the model of where time goes is wrong.

---

6. FRAME PACING AND THE HUMAN LOOP
When a person is in the loop, their own reaction time dominates the
budget.
Human visual reaction is on the order of a few hundred milliseconds,
which usually dwarfs the tens of milliseconds of a machine pipeline.
The consequence is subtle: a system can be measurably slower and still
feel the same, until it is slow enough that its delay composes with the
human's rather than hiding inside it.
Design the machine latency to stay well under the human stage, then
measure the human stage rather than guessing it.

---

7. THREADING, PRIORITY, AND SCHEDULING
Latency is set as much by scheduling as by algorithm.
Key choices:
* how many threads run, and what each one owns.
* whether capture, processing, and output are pipelined or serialised.
* thread priority, and whether the work competes with the game it is
  observing.
Running heavy work on the same resources the game needs increases both
parties' latency; isolating and prioritising the time-critical stage
reduces the tail.

---

8. ADAPTIVE RATE AND BACKPRESSURE
A robust pipeline adapts rather than assuming a fixed frame rate.
Two mechanisms handle overload:
* dropping work deliberately when behind, so latency does not grow
  without bound (a queue that never drains is worse than a dropped
  frame).
* backpressure, where the consumer signals the producer to slow down.
Without one of these, an overloaded pipeline accumulates delay until it
either catches up in a burst or collapses. Bounded queues are the
practical expression of this principle.

---

9. LATENCY IN THE CAPTURE PATH
The capture reference names GPU-to-CPU readback as the usual dominant
cost, and that is a latency statement as much as a cost statement: it is
a synchronisation point where one stage waits for another.
Placing such points deliberately - and counting them in the budget - is
the difference between a pipeline whose latency is understood and one
whose latency is discovered only in complaints.

---

10. PUTTING A BUDGET TOGETHER
A practical method:
1. state the total response time that is acceptable.
2. subtract the human stage, if a person is in the loop.
3. divide the remainder across sense, process, act, and propagate.
4. identify the synchronisation points and buffers, and assign each its
   share of delay.
5. measure end to end against the budget, using an external reference.
When a stage overruns, decide whether to spend latency elsewhere, drop
work adaptively, or redesign that stage - in that order of preference.

---

11. SUMMARY
Latency, throughput, and jitter are distinct, and perception-and-react
systems care most about latency and its tail.
Delay is mostly waiting: queues, buffers, batching, and synchronisation
points, each of which buys smoothness with time.
Budgets are built by subtraction and validated by external measurement,
and robustness comes from bounded queues and adaptive rate rather than
from assuming a fixed cadence.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
Latency is a budget, not a property. It is divided across stages that are
in series, spent on buffers that trade smoothness for delay, and hidden
in synchronisation points like GPU readback. Improve the bottleneck,
bound the queues, measure end to end - and remember that when a human is
in the loop, their reaction time is usually the largest line item, so it
is the one to measure rather than assume.
