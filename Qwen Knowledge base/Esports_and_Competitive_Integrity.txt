KNOWLEDGE BASE: ESPORTS AND COMPETITIVE INTEGRITY

Purpose:
Explain how integrity is protected in organised competition, where the
stakes, the setting, and the threat model differ from ordinary online
play. It complements BAN GOVERNANCE AND IDENTITY INTEGRITY, MATCHMAKING
AND ACCOUNT ECONOMY INTEGRITY, and APEX LEGENDS ANTI-CHEAT AND THE
PROVIDER CHANGE.

This document is reference knowledge. It describes competitive-integrity
practices from a defensive and organisational perspective, using publicly
documented practice. It contains no instructions for wrongdoing in any
competition.

Practices vary by game and change over time, so treat each statement as a
documented CLAIM. Details are given "as of October 2026".

Related documents: BAN GOVERNANCE AND IDENTITY INTEGRITY, MATCHMAKING
AND ACCOUNT ECONOMY INTEGRITY, HOW ANTI-CHEATS WORK - VANGUARD,
BATTLTEYE, EAC, AND JAVELIN, APEX LEGENDS ANTI-CHEAT AND THE PROVIDER
CHANGE, and the numbered technical series.

---

1. WHY ESPORTS IS A DIFFERENT PROBLEM
In esports the stakes are reputation, prize money, and careers, not just a
match.
That raises the incentive to cheat and the cost of getting it wrong.
It also changes who can act: organisers control the environment far more
than in casual play.
Integrity therefore becomes an organisational discipline, not only a
technical one.

---

2. ON-SITE VERSUS ONLINE INTEGRITY
Competition happens either at a controlled venue or over the internet.
On-site play lets organisers control hardware, network, and observation.
Online play does not, so it must rely on software and monitoring.
The two settings have genuinely different threat models.

---

3. THE LAN ADVANTAGE
At a LAN, the organiser owns the machines and the network.
This removes entire cheat classes, such as many hardware and
network-layer attacks.
It also makes observation possible from outside the player's machine.
Control of the environment is the strongest integrity tool esports has.

---

4. PROVIDED HARDWARE AND PERIPHERALS
Events often supply machines, mice, keyboards, and headsets.
Provided hardware blocks personal devices that could carry hidden
capability.
It also standardises performance, making competition fairer.
The cost is that players lose familiar equipment, which is accepted in
exchange for trust.

---

5. REFEREES AND OBSERVERS
Human referees watch matches and review footage.
Observers see the game state from the organiser's side, as players do.
People catch things that statistics miss, and vice versa.
Human and automated integrity work best together.

---

6. THE DELAY AND INFORMATION PROBLEM
Live competition must balance transparency against information leakage.
Broadcast delays stop real-time information reaching competitors.
Rules govern who may see what and when.
Integrity here is about information control as much as about cheating.

---

7. MATCH FIXING AND BETTING FRAUD
Where money is wagered, the incentive is not to win but to profit from a
chosen result.
This links competition to gambling and occasionally to organised crime.
It is addressed by monitoring betting markets and investigating anomalies.
Match-fixing is an integrity problem that no anti-cheat product
addresses.

---

8. COACHING AND COMMUNICATION RULES
Rules govern who may communicate with players during a match.
Unauthorised information can be as decisive as a cheating program.
Integrity therefore covers the communication channel, not only the game.
This is another area where policy, not software, does the work.

---

9. INTEGRITY OF THE COMPETITION ITSELF
Not all threats are from players; some are from organisers, staff, or
officials.
Conflicts of interest and access to results are integrity risks.
Good governance separates roles and adds oversight.
The competition's own structure must be trustworthy.

---

10. PLAYER CONTRACTS AND RULES
Contracts and rulebooks define what counts as misconduct and what the
penalties are.
They give decisions a basis and an appeal path.
Without them, enforcement looks arbitrary and invites dispute.
Rules turn integrity from an opinion into a process.

---

11. WHY ONLINE ESPORTS STRUGGLES
Online competition cannot control hardware or the network.
It leans on client anti-cheat, monitoring, and delayed enforcement.
That leaves a gap relative to LAN play, which organisers try to narrow
with verification and observation.
The gap is structural, not a failure of effort.

---

12. SUMMARY
Esports integrity is organisational and technical at once, because the
stakes and the setting differ from casual play.
LAN play removes hardware and network cheat classes by controlling the
environment.
Provided hardware, referees, and broadcast delays each address a specific
risk.
Match fixing, coaching leaks, and official misconduct are integrity
problems beyond any anti-cheat.
Rules, contracts, and appeals turn integrity decisions into a process.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
Competitive integrity is won by controlling the environment, not just the
software. At a LAN the organiser owns the network, the hardware, and the
observation, which removes whole cheat classes at once; online, that
control is absent and must be approximated with verification and delay.
Beyond cheating lie match fixing and governance risks that no product
addresses. Protect the competition's structure as carefully as its
clients.
