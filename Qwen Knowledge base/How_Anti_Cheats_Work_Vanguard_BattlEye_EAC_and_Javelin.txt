KNOWLEDGE BASE: HOW ANTI-CHEATS WORK - VANGUARD, BATTLTEYE, EAC, AND JAVELIN

Purpose:
Educational reference explaining the shared anatomy of a modern PC game
anti-cheat, then how four well-known products - Riot Vanguard, BattlEye,
Easy Anti-Cheat (EAC), and EA Javelin (with its EAAC roots) - each
implement that anatomy in practice. It covers what each loads, when it
loads, what it observes, what it trusts, how it decides, and how it
enforces, so the four can be compared by mechanism rather than by
reputation.

This document is reference knowledge. It describes systems from a
defensive and engineering perspective, using only publicly documented
facts and the vendors' own public statements. It deliberately contains
no recipes for defeating, evading, or bypassing any product, and no
operational detail beyond what the vendors themselves publish.

These products change frequently and disclose only part of their design,
so treat every statement here as a publicly documented CLAIM, not as a
verified internal fact. Details are given "as of October 2026".

Related documents: ANTI-CHEAT ARCHITECTURE, ANTI-CHEAT PRODUCTS AND
SYSTEMS, KERNEL MODE AND ANTI-CHEAT DRIVERS, SERVER-AUTHORITATIVE
ARCHITECTURE AND TRUST BOUNDARIES, HARDWARE, DMA, AND BELOW-KERNEL
THREATS, APEX LEGENDS ANTI-CHEAT AND THE PROVIDER CHANGE, MEASURING
ANTI-CHEAT EFFECTIVENESS, EVADING ANTI-CHEAT - CATEGORIES AND
COUNTERMEASURES, and the numbered technical series.

1. WHAT "HOW IT WORKS" MEANS HERE
"How it works" does not mean reverse-engineering instructions.
It means the same five questions ANTI-CHEAT PRODUCTS AND SYSTEMS uses:
where the observer runs, when it loads, what it trusts, how it decides,
and how it enforces.
Answering those five for a product tells you almost everything that
matters about how it behaves in practice.
The four products below are four different sets of answers to the same
five questions.

---

2. THE SHARED ANATOMY OF A MODERN ANTI-CHEAT
Nearly every serious PC anti-cheat is assembled from the same parts:
* a client agent that runs with the game.
* a privileged component - usually a kernel-mode driver - that observes
  and constrains what user mode cannot.
* a backend that receives telemetry and decides.
* a detection engine: signatures, behavioural heuristics, machine
  learning, or all three.
* an enforcement system: ban waves, kicks, hardware or account bans, or
  live mitigation.
The differences between products are mostly differences in which part
they emphasise and what they choose to trust.

---

3. THE SESSION LIFECYCLE, SHARED IN OUTLINE
Almost all of them follow the same timeline:
boot or launch, driver or agent load, handshake with the game and
backend, continuous observation, telemetry to the backend, a decision,
and then enforcement.
Where a product sits on this timeline - especially how early it loads -
sets what it can guarantee about everything that happened before it.
A product that loads at boot sees the whole session from the start;
one that loads with the game sees only from that point.
That single timing choice explains many of the visible differences
below.

---

4. COUNTER-FORENSICS: WHY ENFORCEMENT IS OFTEN DELAYED
Detection and enforcement are deliberately separated.
If a cheat is banned the instant it is detected, its author learns what
was detected and can adapt the very next day.
Delayed ban waves let one detection catch many users of the same cheat
before its author can react.
This is why "detected" and "banned" are not the same moment, and why a
cheat can appear to "work" while it is already flagged.
The same logic drives server-side-only detection, where the client is
never told what triggered a flag.

---

5. RIOT VANGUARD - HOW IT WORKS
Vendor: Riot Games, first shipped broadly with VALORANT (2020).
Vanguard has two parts: a user-mode client that runs with the game, and
a kernel-mode driver plus a service that installs on the machine.
Its defining choice is timing: the driver loads at boot and stays
resident, so it is present before the game launches and even when the
game is not running.
Because it is already loaded, it can establish a baseline it trusts
before any other software has had a chance to interfere.
Vanguard requires the machine to be in a known-good boot state.
On Windows 11, VALORANT requires TPM 2.0 and Secure Boot, and the client
also checks for Virtualization-Based Security and OS exploit protection.
Riot publishes specific error codes for these, such as VAN 9001 (Secure
Boot) and codes for missing TPM or VBS, so a blocked machine is told
exactly which requirement failed.
Vanguard also maintains and updates a blocklist of known-vulnerable
drivers and refuses to let them load, closing a common way of reaching
the kernel.
The kernel driver is Windows-only; there is no Linux or macOS driver.
What it trusts: the boot chain and hardware measurements - the part of
the machine a cheat running underneath the game cannot easily forge.
Trade-off it illustrates: maximum visibility into early-boot and
low-level activity, bought with a driver that lives on every machine at
all times, which is exactly what draws the most privacy criticism.

---

6. BATTLTEYE - HOW IT WORKS
Vendor: BattlEye Innovations (Germany), independent since 2004, and
originally created for the Battlefield series.
BattlEye combines proactive kernel-based protection with dynamic
detection rather than one fixed scan.
Its distinctive mechanism is server-directed scanning: the backend
chooses what the client should look at, on the fly.
Because the scan targets are decided centrally and change over time, a
cheat author cannot profile the client once, defeat that single check,
and assume safety.
It also has a server component that keeps control of the server side and
can kick clients, plus administrative tooling for operators.
Its global ban system is tied to account identity (for example a
SteamID), and the vendor claims its ban reach rivals or exceeds that of
Valve's VAC bans.
Used by: PUBG, Rainbow Six Siege, the Arma and DayZ lineage, and many
others.
What it trusts: its own kernel module plus a backend it controls, rather
than requiring a hardware-attested boot the way Vanguard does.
Trade-off it illustrates: a mostly proactive kernel defender, with a
strong server side and consolidated, identity-linked global bans.

---

7. EASY ANTI-CHEAT (EAC) - HOW IT WORKS
Vendor: Epic Games, via its 2018 acquisition of the Finnish firm Kamu,
and now part of Epic Online Services.
EAC is modular and tiered rather than one fixed configuration.
A title can run it with only the lighter, user-mode level of protection,
or add a kernel-mode component for higher-risk competitive games.
That choice belongs to the publisher, so two EAC-protected games can be
protected to quite different depths.
It is cross-platform by design: Windows, macOS, Linux, and Steam Deck
(through the Proton compatibility layer), with compatibility for console
crossplay.
The kernel driver, where used, is effectively the Windows component; the
other platforms lean on the lighter layer.
It integrates tightly with Epic Online Services for accounts and
matchmaking, which is another reason large publishers adopt it.
What it trusts: its own client and driver plus a strong tie-in to Epic's
platform; by default it does not demand a hardware-attested boot the way
Vanguard does.
Used by a very large share of PC multiplayer, including Rust and
Fortnite, and Apex Legends from its 2019 launch until the September 2026
switch away from it.
Trade-off it illustrates: standardisation and scale - one vendor,
integrated with a platform, serving many titles across many operating
systems, with a protection depth that varies per game.

---

8. EA JAVELIN (AND ITS EAAC ROOTS) - HOW IT WORKS
Vendor: Electronic Arts, as a first-party (in-house) system.
It began in September 2022 as EA Anti-Cheat (EAAC), EA's own kernel-mode
PC anti-cheat, and was reintroduced under the "EA Javelin Anticheat"
brand in April 2025 as the evolution of that same system.
It is kernel-mode, and EA states plainly that it runs at the kernel
level because detecting external cheats requires it.
Its model is game-scoped rather than boot-resident: EA states Javelin
runs only while a Javelin-protected game is running, and uninstalls
itself from a machine once no protected games remain installed.
EA frames it as part of a "defense-in-depth" strategy and publishes
accuracy figures - a claimed detection accuracy above 99% and a
false-positive rate below 1%.
A September 2026 EA progress report cites 20 protected titles, 57.1
million players, and 26.7 million cheat attempts prevented over the
year.
It protects a growing list of EA titles, including EA SPORTS FC, Madden
NFL, the Battlefield series (and Battlefield 6), F1, WRC, Plants vs.
Zombies, skate., and - from September 29, 2026 - Apex Legends.
What it trusts: EA's own client, driver, telemetry, and backend; it is
first-party like Vanguard, but it does not make Secure Boot a hard
requirement for every title (Apex, for example, did not require it).
Trade-off it illustrates: a first-party system tuned to EA's own engines
and threat model, at the cost of running EA's privileged code on players'
machines and depending on EA alone for detections.

---

9. THE FOUR SIDE BY SIDE
A compact comparison on the five questions, using public claims only:
VANGUARD
* where it runs: user-mode client plus a kernel driver.
* when it loads: at boot, and it stays resident.
* what it trusts: hardware and boot measurements (TPM, Secure Boot).
* how it decides: kernel observation, machine learning, and a
  vulnerable-driver blocklist.
* how it enforces: account and hardware bans, in waves.
* ownership: first-party (Riot), Windows kernel driver only.
BATTLTEYE
* where it runs: kernel mode, plus a server component.
* when it loads: with the game, and can keep a persistent presence.
* what it trusts: its own kernel module and backend.
* how it decides: proactive protection plus backend-directed dynamic
  scanning.
* how it enforces: global account-linked bans and server kicks.
* ownership: third-party vendor, independent.
EASY ANTI-CHEAT
* where it runs: user mode plus an optional kernel component.
* when it loads: with the game.
* what it trusts: its own client/driver and the Epic platform.
* how it decides: signatures, heuristics, and backend analysis, tiered
  per title.
* how it enforces: publisher-configured bans.
* ownership: third-party vendor (Epic), cross-platform.
JAVELIN
* where it runs: kernel mode, plus client and backend.
* when it loads: with a protected game; self-uninstalls when none
  remain.
* what it trusts: EA's own client, driver, telemetry, and backend.
* how it decides: in-house detections over aggregated EA telemetry and
  data.
* how it enforces: EA account bans across EA titles.
* ownership: first-party (EA), EA titles only.

---

10. THE TIMING AXIS: BOOT-RESIDENT VERSUS GAME-SCOPED
The sharpest real difference among these four is when the privileged
component is present.
Vanguard is boot-resident: it is on the machine from startup, so its
trust baseline predates the game.
Javelin is game-scoped: it is present while a protected game runs and
removes itself once none remain.
BattlEye and EAC load with the game, though BattlEye can keep a
persistent presence in practice.
Boot-residency buys the strongest guarantee about what happened before
the game, at the highest ongoing cost in privilege.
Game-scoped operation is a smaller footprint on the machine, at the cost
of a later baseline.
Neither is "better"; they are two points on the same trade-off curve.

---

11. THE TRUST AXIS: WHAT EACH RELIES ON
The second sharp difference is what the system ultimately trusts.
Vanguard pushes trust down to hardware and boot measurements a cheat
running beneath the game cannot easily forge.
BattlEye trusts its own kernel module plus a backend it fully controls.
EAC trusts its own client/driver and its integration with the Epic
platform.
Javelin trusts EA's own client, driver, telemetry, and backend.
The whole field is trending toward the Vanguard end: adding Secure Boot,
TPM, and attestation so trust rests on the hardware rather than on
software an attacker might already control.

---

12. THE OWNERSHIP AXIS: THIRD-PARTY VERSUS FIRST-PARTY
BattlEye and EAC are third-party vendors: one product, many publishers,
tuned to a shared baseline.
Vanguard and Javelin are first-party: built and owned by the company
that also owns the game, its accounts, and - for Riot and EA - its
esports.
First-party systems can be tuned to one engine and one threat model and
can fuse detection with the publisher's own account and matchmaking
data.
Third-party systems bring scale, cross-platform reach, and expertise
across many titles.
This axis, rather than any single feature, is why publishers switch
providers in either direction.

---

13. WHAT ALL FOUR HAVE IN COMMON
Despite the differences, all four converge on the same decisions:
a privileged kernel component, because a user-mode observer can be
manipulated by something beneath it.
A backend that owns detection and receives telemetry.
A separation between detection and enforcement, usually with delayed
ban waves.
A growing reliance on account and device identity to punish the person,
not just the process.
And an implicit admission that the client alone can never be fully
trusted.

---

14. HOW TO REASON ABOUT ANY ANTI-CHEAT YOU MEET
Run the five questions in order: where does the observer run, when does
it load, what does it ultimately trust, how does it decide, and how does
it enforce.
Then ask the framing question from ANTI-CHEAT PRODUCTS AND SYSTEMS: what
would have to be true about the machine for this product's evidence to
be sound, and who controls that.
Use the timing, trust, and ownership axes above to place any product
relative to these four.
And read SERVER-AUTHORITATIVE ARCHITECTURE AND TRUST BOUNDARIES to see
the part of the problem no client-side product can solve on its own.

---

15. SUMMARY
Vanguard, BattlEye, EAC, and Javelin are the same anatomy in different
proportions: a client agent, a privileged component, a backend, a
detection engine, and an enforcement system.
They differ mainly on three axes: timing (Vanguard is boot-resident,
while Javelin, EAC, and largely BattlEye load with the game), trust
(Vanguard leans on hardware and boot measurements; the others on their
own kernel module and backend), and ownership (Vanguard and Javelin are
first-party; BattlEye and EAC are third-party vendors).
Detection is driven from the backend in all four, and detection is
always separated from enforcement so that ban waves can catch many users
of one cheat before its author reacts.
The kernel component is the shared default because a user-mode observer
can be hidden from or manipulated by code beneath it.
Read any of them by these axes, and by what they trust, rather than by
the brand on the box.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
Every serious PC anti-cheat is one anatomy - client, privileged
component, backend, detection, enforcement - arranged around a single
question: what does it ultimately trust? Vanguard answers with hardware
and boot measurements, BattlEye and EAC with their own kernel module and
backend, and Javelin with EA's own client, driver, and telemetry. Apex
Legends moving from EAC to Javelin was a change on the ownership axis,
not the anatomy: the same layered design, a different owner. Learn the
five questions - where, when, trust, decide, enforce - and any
anti-cheat, present or future, stops being a mysterious brand and
becomes a set of understandable trade-offs.
