KNOWLEDGE BASE: SIGNAL PROCESSING AND FILTERING

Purpose:
Educational reference for the treatment of sampled measurements: how
discrete signals behave, why smoothing costs latency, and how filters
interact with control and tracking. It provides the foundations beneath
the tracking and control references.

---

1. WHAT A SIGNAL IS
A signal is a quantity that varies, usually with time.
In these systems it is sampled: measured at discrete instants and stored
as a sequence of numbers.
Everything downstream reasons about that sequence, not a continuous
reality.

---

2. DISCRETE SAMPLING
Sampling records a value at regular intervals.
The interval and its regularity both matter.
An irregular sampling clock is itself a source of error and must be
accounted for.

---

3. SAMPLING RATE AND THE SAMPLING THEOREM
To represent a phenomenon faithfully, it must be sampled faster than
twice its highest meaningful frequency (the Nyquist rate).
Sampling too slowly loses detail irreversibly.

---

4. ALIASING
A phenomenon faster than half the sampling rate appears as a slower,
false pattern.
This is an alias: an artifact of sampling, not the real signal.
Aliasing cannot be undone after sampling, only prevented before it.

---

5. NOISE VERSUS SIGNAL
Noise is unwanted variation; signal is the variation of interest.
They are often distinguished by how fast they change, which is why
filtering is fundamentally about frequency.

---

6. FREQUENCY AS A VIEWPOINT
Any sampled sequence can be seen as a sum of components at different
rates of change.
Filters act by attenuating some components and preserving others.
Thinking in frequency explains what a filter does before any formula.

---

7. AVERAGING AND ITS EFFECTS
Averaging suppresses fast variation.
The more samples averaged, the smoother the output and the more it lags
behind real change.
Smoothing and responsiveness trade against each other.

---

8. MOVING AVERAGE
A moving average outputs the mean of the last N samples.
It is simple and predictable.
Its lag grows with N, and a single large disturbance biases the whole
window.

---

9. EXPONENTIAL MOVING AVERAGE
An exponential moving average blends the new sample with the previous
output using a weight.
A small weight is smooth but slow; a large weight is responsive but
jumpy.
It needs little memory and is common in real-time systems.

---

10. LOW-PASS FILTERING
A low-pass filter keeps slow changes and attenuates fast ones.
It is the general form of smoothing.
Cutoff frequency sets what "slow" means.

---

11. HIGH-PASS AND BANDPASS
A high-pass filter does the opposite: it keeps fast changes and removes
slow drift.
A bandpass keeps a middle range.
Choosing a band is choosing which behaviour to treat as signal.

---

12. SMOOTHING VERSUS LAG
Every linear filter trades noise reduction against delay.
Less noise means more lag, and lag degrades control and tracking.
This trade-off should be a measured decision, not a default.

---

13. PHASE AND DELAY
Filters shift a signal in time.
That shift is a phase change, and in a control loop it acts like added
latency, which reduces stability margin.
Smoothed sensors are not free.

---

14. FILTER GAIN AND CUTOFF
Gain describes how much each frequency is passed; cutoff marks the
transition.
Filters are characterised by these responses, not only by their code.

---

15. JITTER
Jitter is variation in timing rather than in value.
It produces spurious apparent change even when the underlying quantity
is steady.
Regular timestamps and timestamp-based math reduce it.

---

16. OUTLIERS AND ROBUST FILTERING
A single wrong sample can dominate a mean.
Robust methods reduce the influence of extreme values.
Detection and rejection of outliers is often more valuable than
smoothing everything.

---

17. MEDIAN AND RANK FILTERS
A median filter outputs the middle of the last N samples.
It rejects isolated outliers while preserving edges better than a mean.
Rank-based filters trade some smoothness for robustness.

---

18. HYSTERESIS
Hysteresis uses different thresholds for entering and leaving a state.
It prevents rapid flicker when a value hovers near one threshold.
It is a decision-shaping tool, not a value-smoothing tool.

---

19. DEBOUNCE
Debounce ignores changes shorter than a chosen duration.
It is the temporal cousin of hysteresis and is common for noisy binary
signals.

---

20. DIFFERENTIATION AND NOISE AMPLIFICATION
Estimating a rate of change amplifies high-frequency content.
Differentiating a noisy signal therefore amplifies noise.
This is why derivative terms in control need filtering and why velocity
from position is delicate.

---

21. FILTERS IN THE CONTROL LOOP
A filter inside a feedback loop adds lag and phase shift.
It can trade steady output for reduced stability margin, producing
oscillation.
Filter placement is a control decision, not a cosmetic one.

---

22. FILTERS IN TRACKING
Trackers use filters to combine prediction with noisy measurements.
The filter's assumptions about noise shape the resulting estimate.
Mismatched assumptions cause either sluggishness or jitter.

---

23. CHOOSING A FILTER
State what must be preserved and what must be rejected.
Then pick the simplest filter meeting the noise and lag budget.
Validate against measured data, not by intuition.

---

24. SUMMARY
Filters shape which parts of a signal survive.
Every choice trades noise against lag, and lag interacts with control
and tracking stability.
Reason in terms of frequency, delay, and robustness, and measure the
result.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
Filtering is a deliberate trade, never a free improvement. Smoothing
reduces noise but adds lag and phase shift, which degrade control and
tracking. Choose a filter from what must be preserved, what must be
rejected, and the lag budget, and validate it on real measurements.
