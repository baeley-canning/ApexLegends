KNOWLEDGE BASE: STATISTICS AND EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN

Purpose:
Educational reference for turning measurements into defensible
decisions: how to characterise uncertainty, compare systems fairly, and
choose thresholds when different errors carry different costs. It sits
beneath the model-evaluation material and above raw metrics.

---

1. WHY THIS MATTERS
A single measurement rarely proves anything.
Conclusions about thresholds, models, and defenses depend on how much a
number varies and how confident we can be in it.
Statistics is the discipline of drawing conclusions that survive
variation and limited data.

---

2. POPULATIONS AND SAMPLES
A population is everything of interest; a sample is the subset actually
observed.
Conclusions about the population are inferred from the sample.
If the sample is not representative, the inference is unreliable no
matter how large it is.

---

3. VARIABILITY
Almost every real quantity varies: latency, scores, and outcomes.
Reporting only an average hides variation that may matter more than the
centre.

---

4. SPREAD AND CENTRAL TENDENCY
Mean, median, and mode summarise the centre; the median resists extreme
values.
Standard deviation, variance, and interquartile range summarise spread.
Two systems can share an average and differ completely in consistency,
and consistency is often what matters operationally.

---

5. SAMPLING BIAS
A sample can be skewed by how it was collected.
Collecting data only at convenient times, or only from easy cases,
distorts conclusions.
Bias is a design problem and is not cured by averaging.

---

6. CONFIDENCE INTERVALS
An interval expresses a range of plausible values with a stated
confidence.
It communicates uncertainty in a way a single number cannot.
A point estimate without an interval invites false precision.

---

7. HYPOTHESIS TESTING
A test asks whether observed data are consistent with a stated
assumption.
It produces evidence for or against that assumption, not proof.

---

8. NULL AND ALTERNATIVE HYPOTHESES
The null is the default, often "no effect" or "no difference."
The alternative is what the experiment tries to detect.
The test measures evidence against the null.

---

9. P-VALUES AND THEIR MISUSE
A p-value is the probability of data at least this extreme if the null
were true.
It is not the probability that the null is true, nor that the result is
important.
With enough data, trivial differences become significant, so
significance alone is not a decision.

---

10. EFFECT SIZE
Effect size measures how large a difference is, independent of sample
count.
Significance says a difference probably exists; effect size says whether
it matters.

---

11. TYPE I AND TYPE II ERRORS
A Type I error concludes an effect exists when it does not (false
positive).
A Type II error misses an effect that exists (false negative).
Reducing one usually increases the other; the trade-off should be chosen
deliberately.

---

12. PRECISION AND RECALL AS COSTS
Precision reflects how many flagged items are genuinely positive.
Recall reflects how many positives were found.
Their balance is a decision about which error is more costly, not a
property of the model.

---

13. CLASS IMBALANCE
In many detection settings the event of interest is rare, so positives
are greatly outnumbered by negatives.
Accuracy is then misleading: predicting "no" for everything scores well.
Report per-class metrics, and evaluate with the prevalence of the real
deployment.

---

14. BASE RATES
The base rate is how common the event actually is.
The rarer the event, the more a modest false-positive rate floods the
alerts.
Base rate is often the dominant factor in whether a detector is usable.

---

15. ROC AND PR CURVES
An ROC curve shows true-positive against false-positive rate across
thresholds.
A precision-recall curve is more informative when positives are rare.
Both describe the trade-off; neither chooses a threshold for you.

---

16. THRESHOLD SELECTION
A threshold converts a score into a decision.
It should follow from the relative cost of each error and the base rate,
not from the curve's shape alone.
A defensible threshold is justified by its expected consequences.

---

17. EXPECTED COST OF DECISIONS
Assign costs to false positives and false negatives.
Expected cost = cost(fp) * rate(fp) + cost(fn) * rate(fn).
The best threshold minimises expected cost for the deployment, not some
abstract score.

---

18. CALIBRATION
A calibrated score means a stated probability matches observed frequency.
Well-calibrated scores let costs be combined in a principled way.
Poorly calibrated scores can rank well yet be unusable for decisions.

---

19. EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
A good comparison fixes everything except the variable under test.
Randomise where possible, hold the rest constant, and change one factor
at a time.

---

20. CONFOUNDING
A confounder varies with the factor under study and can masquerade as its
effect.
Latency, load, and time of day are common confounders.
Detecting them is part of designing the experiment.

---

21. OFFLINE AND REPLAY EVALUATION
Replaying recorded data lets systems be compared reproducibly and
cheaply.
If the data came from a different policy, the replay reflects that
policy, not the new system's behaviour in the world.

---

22. SAMPLE SIZE
Too few samples cannot detect a real effect; too many manufacture
trivial significance.
Sample size should follow from the effect size and the confidence
required.

---

23. REPORTING AND REPRODUCIBILITY
Report sample sizes, intervals, and the exact conditions.
A result that cannot be reproduced is a claim, not a finding.

---

24. COMMON PITFALLS
Optimising a metric that does not match the goal.
Testing many hypotheses and reporting only the significant one.
Comparing on a non-representative sample.
Treating a model score as a probability without checking calibration.

---

25. SUMMARY
Statistics does not remove uncertainty; it quantifies and manages it.
Good decisions come from representative data, honest uncertainty,
cost-aware thresholds, and comparisons that isolate the factor under
test.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
A measurement is not a conclusion. Decide using effect sizes, intervals,
and the real base rate, then choose thresholds from the relative cost of
false positives and false negatives. When positives are rare, accuracy
and ROC flatter a useless detector; report per-class metrics and reason
about expected cost.
