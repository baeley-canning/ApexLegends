KNOWLEDGE BASE: TELEMETRY, PRIVACY, AND PLAYER DATA ETHICS

Purpose:
Explain what a modern anti-cheat collects about a player's machine and
behaviour, why it collects it, and the privacy, legal, and ethical
questions that follow. It is the companion to BAN GOVERNANCE AND IDENTITY
INTEGRITY and ANTI-CHEAT ARCHITECTURE, focused on data rather than on
enforcement.

This document is reference knowledge. It describes data flows and their
consequences from a defensive and policy perspective, using publicly
documented practices. It contains no recipe for evading detection or for
attacking any product.

Anti-cheat data practices change often and are only partly disclosed, so
treat each statement as a publicly documented CLAIM, not a verified
internal fact. Details are given "as of October 2026".

Related documents: BAN GOVERNANCE AND IDENTITY INTEGRITY, ANTI-CHEAT
ARCHITECTURE, HOW ANTI-CHEATS WORK - VANGUARD, BATTLTEYE, EAC, AND
JAVELIN, HARDWARE, DMA, AND BELOW-KERNEL THREATS, and the numbered
technical series.

---

1. WHY ANTI-CHEAT COLLECTS DATA AT ALL
Detection needs evidence, and evidence needs observation.
A cheat that leaves no observable trace cannot be detected by any method.
The more powerful the observation, the stronger the evidence, and the
more sensitive the data.
This is the root of the entire privacy tension: effectiveness and
intrusiveness rise together.

---

2. THE KINDS OF DATA COLLECTED
Public descriptions typically include several families of data:
* static machine facts: operating system version, hardware, and installed
  security features.
* identifiers: account, device, and hardware identifiers used to link
  sessions and to enforce bans.
* behavioural telemetry: input timing, aim patterns, and match statistics.
* process and memory observations: which programs run and, in kernel-mode
  systems, what regions of memory are touched.
Each family carries a different privacy weight and a different value to
detection.

---

3. DEVICE AND HARDWARE IDENTIFIERS
To ban a person rather than a single account, a system needs something
more stable than a login.
Hardware and device identifiers serve that purpose and are the backbone
of ban-evasion defence.
The same property that makes them useful - persistence across accounts -
is what makes them sensitive.
See BAN GOVERNANCE AND IDENTITY INTEGRITY for how they are used in
enforcement.

---

4. BEHAVIOURAL AND TELEMETRY DATA
Behavioural data is gathered at scale to find statistical anomalies that
signatures miss.
It is powerful because it survives recompilation and obfuscation of a
cheat.
It is sensitive because it can describe a person's habits, skill, and
play schedule.
Aggregated behavioural data is also hard to interpret without context,
which complicates both detection and privacy review.

---

5. MEMORY AND PROCESS OBSERVATION DATA
Kernel-mode anti-cheats can observe more of the machine than user-mode
ones.
That capability is exactly why they are contentious.
The observation is usually described as pattern-matching for known cheat
markers rather than free-form reading of personal files.
Even so, the technical ability to read process memory is broader than
what any single detection rule uses, and that gap is the heart of the
debate.

---

6. WHERE THE DATA GOES AND HOW LONG IT LASTS
Telemetry generally leaves the machine and is analysed on a backend.
Retention varies and is often only summarised in privacy policies.
Short retention limits exposure; long retention improves investigations.
The trade-off between the two is rarely explained to players in plain
terms.

---

7. DEVICE HISTOGRAMS AND THE CROSS-GAME QUESTION
Some vendors build a device-level profile that persists across many
titles and even across publishers.
This is efficient - one ban propagates - and it is also the most
privacy-sensitive construct in the field.
It raises a question no single game's terms of service cleanly answers:
who owns the reputation of a machine, and for how long.

---

8. THE PRIVACY TENSION, STATED PLAINLY
Players accept observation in exchange for fair matches.
They object when the observation exceeds what the stated threat model
requires, or when it is not disclosed.
The tension is not solvable in the abstract; it is managed by
proportionality, disclosure, and restraint.
A system that collects the minimum it can justify is both more ethical
and easier to defend publicly.

---

9. LEGAL FRAMEWORKS - GDPR, CCPA, AND BEYOND
Data-protection law treats some anti-cheat data as personal data.
Under regimes such as the GDPR, collection needs a lawful basis, a
defined purpose, and a retention limit.
Identifiers and behavioural profiles are the most likely to be regulated.
Compliance is a moving target because the technology and the law both
change quickly.

---

10. CONSENT, TERMS OF SERVICE, AND REAL CONSENT
Anti-cheat data collection is usually covered by a click-through terms of
service.
Legal consent is not the same as informed consent; few players read the
terms they accept.
A regime built entirely on "you agreed" therefore carries an ethical gap.
Disclosure in plain language narrows that gap; boilerplate widens it.

---

11. DATA MINIMISATION AND PURPOSE LIMITATION
Two principles constrain good collection practice.
Data minimisation: gather only what the detection purpose requires.
Purpose limitation: do not reuse anti-cheat data for unrelated purposes
such as marketing or monetisation.
Both are simple to state and hard to prove from the outside.
They are also the strongest defence a vendor can offer against overreach
accusations.

---

12. SECURITY OF THE COLLECTED DATA
Collected data must itself be protected.
A store of device identifiers and behavioural profiles is a valuable
target and a potential privacy breach.
Encryption, access control, and retention limits apply to anti-cheat data
exactly as they do to any sensitive dataset.
A system that observes intrusively but protects poorly has inverted its
own priorities.

---

13. TRANSPARENCY AND DISCLOSURE
Some vendors publish transparency reports describing what they collect
and how often they enforce.
Transparency does not remove the trade-off, but it makes it reviewable.
It also builds the trust that makes acceptance of intrusive observation
possible.
Opacity, by contrast, feeds the suspicion that the system does more than
it admits.

---

14. THE ETHICS OF KERNEL-LEVEL OBSERVATION
Kernel-mode observation is the strongest tool available and the hardest
to justify when it exceeds the stated need.
The ethical test is proportionality: does the capability match a real,
disclosed threat?
Restraint - collecting less, retaining less, disclosing more - is the
practical answer.
The technical justification for kernel access is genuine; the ethical
burden is to use only the part that is necessary.

---

15. SUMMARY
Anti-cheat collects data because detection requires evidence, and the
evidence that catches cheats is often the same data that is sensitive.
The families are machine facts, identifiers, behavioural telemetry, and
process and memory observations, each with its own privacy weight.
Device identifiers drive ban enforcement; behavioural data drives
statistical detection; memory observation drives kernel-mode detection.
The privacy tension is real and is managed by proportionality,
disclosure, and restraint rather than solved outright.
Law sets a floor; ethics sets the standard the law does not reach.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
Anti-cheat data gathering sits on a single trade-off: the observation
powerful enough to catch cheats is the observation sensitive enough to
alarm players. Every design question - what to collect, how long to keep
it, whom to share it with - is a point on that trade-off. The defensible
position is not "collect nothing" or "collect everything", but to collect
the minimum the disclosed threat model justifies, keep it only as long as
need requires, and say so plainly.
