KNOWLEDGE BASE: REAL-TIME SYSTEMS ENGINEERING

Purpose:
Educational reference for building systems that must respond within time
constraints: how to structure capture, inference, and action as a
pipeline, how to manage latency and load, and how to keep behaviour
predictable.

---

1. REAL-TIME DOES NOT MEAN FAST
A real-time system is one whose correctness depends on meeting timing
constraints.
Being fast is not the same as being predictable.
A slow but consistent system can be more useful than a fast irregular
one.

---

2. DEADLINES AND DETERMINISM
A deadline is the latest acceptable time to complete work.
Determinism is how repeatable the timing is.
Real-time engineering seeks to bound the worst case, not only improve
the average.

---

3. HARD, FIRM, SOFT
Hard: missing a deadline is a failure.
Firm: a late result is useless but not catastrophic.
Soft: a late result is degraded but still useful.
Classifying the requirement decides how much effort is justified.

---

4. THE PIPELINE AS A CHAIN OF DEADLINES
Capture, preprocess, infer, postprocess, track, decide, and act each
consume time.
The end-to-end deadline is satisfied only if the stages fit within it.
Every stage and transfer is a candidate bottleneck.

---

5. PRODUCER-CONSUMER
One component produces frames; another consumes them.
Decoupling them prevents a slow consumer from blocking the producer, at
the cost of a buffer.

---

6. BUFFERS AND QUEUES
A buffer smooths bursts but adds delay.
A queue that grows without bound converts a latency problem into a
memory problem and hides overload.
Buffer size is a latency decision.

---

7. FRAME DROP POLICY
When the pipeline cannot keep up, some frames must be discarded.
Dropping the oldest frame keeps results fresh; dropping the newest
preserves order at the cost of staleness.
The policy should be explicit and matched to the goal.

---

8. BACKPRESSURE
Backpressure is signalling a producer to slow down when the consumer
cannot keep up.
Ignoring it leads to ever-growing queues and rising latency.
Applying it keeps the system stable under overload.

---

9. LATENCY VERSUS THROUGHPUT
Throughput is work per unit time; latency is delay per item.
They can be improved independently and sometimes trade against each
other.
Batching improves throughput while increasing latency.

---

10. BATCHING
Processing several items together amortises fixed costs.
It raises per-item latency, which may be unacceptable in a control loop.

---

11. BLOCKING VERSUS NON-BLOCKING
Blocking waits for a result; non-blocking proceeds and checks later.
Blocking code is simpler but can stall an entire pipeline.
Non-blocking code is more complex but keeps stages decoupled.

---

12. THREADS
Threads run concurrently and share memory.
They suit I/O-bound wait and can overlap stages.
Sharing memory requires synchronization, and oversubscription causes
context-switching overhead.

---

13. ASYNCIO
An event loop multiplexes many waits on one thread.
It suits many concurrent I/O operations and avoids lock contention.
It does not speed up CPU-bound work and can be blocked by a single
long computation.

---

14. PROCESSES
Processes isolate memory and can use multiple cores for CPU-bound work.
They avoid the constraints of a shared interpreter, at the cost of
communication and startup overhead.

---

15. SYNCHRONIZATION AND CONTENTION
Locks and shared state create waiting.
Contention converts parallelism into serialization and adds jitter.
Prefer message passing and immutable data where practical.

---

16. SCHEDULING AND PRIORITY
The operating system decides when threads run.
Priorities and affinity influence timeliness but are not guarantees.
Jitter from scheduling is a real source of missed deadlines.

---

17. TIMESTAMPS AND FRAME AGE
Every stage should know when its input was captured.
Frame age is the gap between capture and use, and it grows under load.
Acting on stale data is a silent cause of poor behaviour.

---

18. THE INTERPRETER AND CONCURRENCY
In Python, the global interpreter lock limits true parallel execution of
pure-Python threads.
CPU-bound work should use processes or native libraries; I/O-bound work
suits threads or asyncio.

---

19. MEASUREMENT AND PROFILING
Measure per-stage timing, not just end-to-end.
Report percentiles, not only averages.
The slowest stage and the slowest case together determine the
experience.

---

20. ADAPTIVE RATE
A system can lower its own frame rate or resolution under load.
Adaptation trades fidelity for timeliness deliberately.

---

21. DETERMINISTIC REPLAY
Recording inputs and timestamps lets a run be replayed identically.
Replay makes regressions reproducible and comparisons fair.

---

22. TESTING REAL-TIME CODE
Test the pipeline with synthetic loads and worst-case inputs.
Verify behaviour under overload, not only in the happy path.

---

23. FAILURE MODES
Unbounded queues, hidden blocking, and unmeasured jitter are the common
ways real-time systems fail while appearing to work.

---

24. SUMMARY
Real-time engineering is about bounded, predictable timing.
Structure the pipeline with explicit buffers and drop policies, measure
per-stage latency and its variation, and keep the system stable under
overload.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
Real-time means timely, not merely fast. Design the pipeline around
deadlines and predictable worst-case behaviour, make buffering and frame
dropping explicit, and measure per-stage latency with percentiles.
Unbounded queues and unmeasured jitter are the most common ways a
working-looking system still fails.
