KNOWLEDGE BASE: SOFTWARE ARCHITECTURE AND DESIGN

Purpose:
Educational reference for structuring software so it is understandable,
testable, and changeable: how to divide responsibility, control
dependencies, and make boundaries explicit.

---

1. WHY ARCHITECTURE
Architecture is the set of decisions that are expensive to change later.
It shapes how easily the system can be understood, tested, and extended.
Its purpose is to keep change affordable over time.

---

2. SEPARATION OF CONCERNS
Each part of a system should address one concern.
Mixing capture, perception, control, and I/O in one module makes every
change risky.
Separation lets each concern evolve independently.

---

3. MODULARITY AND COHESION
A module should hold closely related responsibilities (high cohesion).
Unrelated responsibilities bundled together reduce clarity and reuse.
Cohesion is about what belongs together.

---

4. COUPLING
Coupling is how much modules depend on each other's details.
Loose coupling means a change in one rarely forces a change in another.
Low coupling and high cohesion together make systems maintainable.

---

5. INTERFACES AND BOUNDARIES
An interface defines what a module offers without exposing how it works.
Boundaries mark where assumptions are checked and where implementations
can be swapped.
Explicit boundaries are where complexity is contained.

---

6. DEPENDENCY DIRECTION
Dependencies should point from volatile, high-level policy toward
stable, low-level mechanisms.
Reversing this makes core logic depend on details, which is fragile.

---

7. DEPENDENCY INJECTION
Instead of creating its own dependencies, a component receives them.
This makes collaborations explicit and replaceable in tests.
It turns hidden coupling into visible wiring.

---

8. INVERSION OF CONTROL
Control over flow is given to a framework or caller rather than
hard-coded.
This decouples high-level logic from the mechanics that invoke it.

---

9. PORTS AND ADAPTERS
Define the core in terms of interfaces (ports) and implement them with
replaceable adapters.
The core does not know whether it talks to a real camera, a file, or a
test double.
This isolates the domain from its environment.

---

10. LAYERING
Organise code into layers with clear responsibilities and allowed
dependency directions.
Layers prevent a change deep in the system from rippling everywhere.

---

11. PIPELINES AND STAGES
A linear flow of stages is easy to reason about and to measure.
Each stage has one job and a clear input and output.
Pipelines align naturally with real-time constraints.

---

12. CONFIGURATION AND PARAMETERS
Values that vary by environment belong in configuration, not in code.
Load and validate once, then pass the result around explicitly.

---

13. FAIL FAST AT BOUNDARIES
Validate inputs where they enter the system and raise clear errors.
Failing early localises the fault instead of propagating corrupt state.
Silent fallbacks hide the real problem.

---

14. VALIDATION AND CONTRACTS
A contract states what a component expects and guarantees.
Checking it at boundaries makes violations obvious.
Well-defined contracts let components be developed and tested
separately.

---

15. ERROR HANDLING AS A DESIGN CHOICE
Decide, per boundary, whether to retry, degrade, or abort.
Handling every error the same way is rarely right.
Error policy belongs in the design, not in scattered try blocks.

---

16. STATELESS VERSUS STATEFUL
Stateless components are easier to reason about and to test.
State should be explicit and owned where it is needed, not scattered
globally.

---

17. IMMUTABILITY
Data that does not change after creation avoids whole classes of bugs.
Where possible, pass values rather than sharing mutable structures.

---

18. OBSERVABILITY AS A DESIGN CONCERN
Decide what to log and measure while designing, not after shipping.
Components should expose their behaviour, not hide it.

---

19. TESTABILITY AND SEAMS
A seam is a place where behaviour can be substituted for testing.
Designing seams up front makes testing possible without contorting the
code.

---

20. REFACTORING
Improve structure in small, behaviour-preserving steps.
Tests make refactoring safe; without them it is rewriting.

---

21. YAGNI AND PREMATURE ABSTRACTION
Do not build for imagined future needs.
Abstraction should follow real, repeated variation, not speculation.

---

22. DOCUMENTING DECISIONS
Record why a choice was made, not only what it is.
The reasoning is what future readers lack.

---

23. SUMMARY
Architecture keeps a system changeable by separating concerns,
controlling dependencies, and making boundaries explicit.
Cohesion, coupling, and dependency direction are the levers; tests and
seams make change safe.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
Good architecture minimises the cost of the next change. Separate
concerns, point dependencies toward stable abstractions, make boundaries
explicit, and validate at them. Prefer simple, honest structures that
are easy to test and to observe over clever ones that are hard to
change.
