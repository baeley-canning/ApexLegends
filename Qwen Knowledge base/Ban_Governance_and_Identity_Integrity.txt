KNOWLEDGE BASE: BAN GOVERNANCE AND IDENTITY INTEGRITY

Purpose:
Educational reference for what happens after detection: how infractions
affect accounts and devices, how identity and device linkage work, how
ban evasion is countered, and how honesty, privacy, and due process
constrain the whole process.

This document is reference knowledge. It describes mechanisms, their
consequences, and how to reason about failures. It is written from a
defensive, engineering, and policy perspective, and uses only publicly
documented practices.

Related documents: ANTI-CHEAT ARCHITECTURE, ANTI-CHEAT PRODUCTS AND
SYSTEMS, SERVER-AUTHORITATIVE ARCHITECTURE AND TRUST BOUNDARIES,
TELEMETRY, PRIVACY, AND PLAYER DATA ETHICS, MATCHMAKING AND ACCOUNT
ECONOMY INTEGRITY, CHEAT ECONOMICS AND THE UNDERGROUND MARKET,
SPOOFING AND BAN EVASION, and the numbered technical series.

---

1. DETECTION IS NOT ENFORCEMENT
The products reference stresses that detection and enforcement are
separate stages.
This document is about the second stage.
A detection is a signal; a ban is a decision about a person, applied
through systems of identity, reputation, and appeals.
Getting the detection right is only half the problem; applying it
consistently and reversibly is the other half, and it is where most of
the harm to a legitimate player, and most of the attacker's opportunity,
actually lives.

---

2. THE ENFORCEMENT TOOLKIT
Enforcement actions, from lightest to heaviest:
* warnings and advisories.
* match-level mitigations (disabling a cheater's effect on others).
* temporary suspensions.
* permanent account bans.
* hardware or device bans.
* global bans across titles and platforms (rare and partner-dependent).
The heavier the action, the more confidence it demands, because its cost
of error rises with it and its reversibility falls.

---

3. DELAYED BANS AND BAN WAVES
Many systems detect on one schedule and enforce on another.
Delaying enforcement:
* lets a detection mature before acting, reducing false positives.
* avoids signalling to attackers exactly which behaviour tripped a
  detector.
* lets the operator ban a cohort at once, which is harder to learn from
  and cheapens any single detection.
The trade-off is that cheaters keep playing during the delay, so the
timing is a deliberate balance between certainty and harm.

---

4. WHAT A BAN ACTUALLY ADDRESSES
An account ban removes access to an account; it does not remove the
person.
If creating a new account is cheap, an account-only ban is temporary.
This is why enforcement escalates to properties that are harder to
change: payment instruments, phone numbers, and hardware fingerprints.
Each escalation increases friction for cheaters and, unavoidably, some
friction for legitimate players.

---

5. DEVICE AND HARDWARE IDENTIFICATION
A "hardware ban" is really a ban of a set of device identifiers and
their combination (sometimes called a fingerprint).
Publicly, vendors describe combining many signals - not one serial
number - so that no single, easily spoofed value decides the outcome.
The engineering tension is explicit:
* too stable an identifier is a privacy problem and a target for
  spoofing.
* too volatile an identifier produces false positives when hardware or
  software changes.
The design goal is a fingerprint that stays stable for one legitimate
user and costs a cheater to vary.

---

6. BAN EVASION
Evasion is the arms race at the identity layer rather than at the
detection layer: new accounts, altered identifiers, spoofed hardware,
shared devices, and purchased or stolen accounts.
Defenses include linking accounts and devices into a graph, weighting
signals by how hard they are to change, and reactively banning accounts
that cluster with known-bad ones.
Because a shared device or household can legitimately produce similar
signals, this is a place where false positives concentrate, and where
appeals matter most.

---

7. FALSE POSITIVES AND THEIR COST
A false positive is a legitimate player punished for resembling a
cheater.
Its costs are asymmetric, and permanent in a way the cheater's are not:
* an unjust ban can lose a paying, long-term customer outright.
* a hardware ban can remove access for everyone sharing a device.
* the reputational cost of a visible mistake exceeds the value of one
  caught cheater.
This asymmetry is why mature systems prefer high-confidence, delayed
enforcement over fast, aggressive action, and why they measure false
positives with the same seriousness as detections.

---

8. APPEALS AND DUE PROCESS
Because errors happen, the enforcement system needs a path back:
* a way for a banned player to be heard.
* human review for contested or high-stakes cases.
* a record of why an action was taken, sufficient to review it.
A system that cannot distinguish "we are confident" from "we are
uncertain" cannot offer due process, and due process is what separates
enforcement from punishment without recourse.

---

9. PRIVACY AND LEGAL CONSTRAINTS
Enforcement touches personal data and crosses jurisdictions:
* device and hardware identifiers are personal data in many regimes, and
  their collection must be justified and disclosed.
* cross-title or cross-platform sharing raises consent and contractual
  questions.
* some jurisdictions and platform policies limit what may be collected,
  or how long it may be kept.
These constraints shape what enforcement is allowed to do, and they are
part of the design rather than an afterthought. The ethics-and-scope
material extends this theme.

---

10. TRANSPARENCY AND COMMUNICATION
Because enforcement is a decision about a person, how it is communicated
matters as much as what is decided:
* telling players what is and is not allowed, before, not after.
* communicating enforcement honestly - including when a detection is a
  claim, not a certainty.
* explaining the appeal path so it can actually be used.
Opaque enforcement erodes trust even when it is correct; transparent
enforcement survives the occasional mistake.

---

11. SUMMARY
Detection produces a signal; enforcement turns it into a decision about
an account, a device, and a person, and the two must stay separate.
Enforcement escalates from mitigations to account and hardware bans, and
lighter actions demand less confidence because they are more reversible.
Identity is really a fingerprint of many signals, chosen to be stable for
one user and costly for a cheater to vary, and ban evasion is the arms
race at that layer.
False positives cost more than missed cheaters, so due process, appeals,
and privacy limits are part of the design.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
A ban is a decision about a person, and that is a heavier thing than a
detection. Enforce with the least confidence you have, delay until
certain, prefer the reversible action to the permanent one, and give
everyone a path back - because the cost of punishing a legitimate player
is not symmetric to the value of catching one cheater. Build the identity
layer to be stable for the honest and expensive for the evader, and never
let detection and enforcement be the same act.
