KNOWLEDGE BASE: MATHEMATICS FOR PERCEPTION AND CONTROL

Purpose:
Educational reference for the mathematical tools that the perception,
tracking, control, and learning documents depend on: linear algebra,
calculus and optimisation, and probability.

---

1. WHY MATHEMATICS
Perception maps measurements to a world state; control maps error to
action; learning maps data to parameters.
Each of those is a mathematical relationship, so the tools below recur
throughout the series.
The goal here is fluency with the ideas, not formal proof.

---

2. NUMBERS AND PRECISION
Real-world quantities are represented as finite-precision numbers.
Integers are exact within range; floating-point numbers are approximate
and cannot represent most decimal values exactly.
Small errors accumulate, so results should be compared with tolerances,
never for exact equality.

---

3. VECTORS
A vector is an ordered list of numbers representing a point, a direction,
or a set of measurements.
Vector addition combines displacements; scaling changes magnitude.
Distances and angles between vectors are the basis of geometry in several
dimensions.

---

4. MATRICES AND LINEAR MAPS
A matrix represents a linear transformation: it maps one vector to
another.
Rotation, scaling, and projection are all matrices.
Composing transformations means multiplying their matrices, which is why
linear algebra underlies camera geometry and coordinate changes.

---

5. COORDINATE FRAMES AND TRANSFORMS
A point's coordinates are meaningless without the frame they are measured
in.
Changing frames means applying a translation and a rotation - an affine
transform.
Most projection and overlay errors trace back to mixing frames.

---

6. CALCULUS AND RATES OF CHANGE
A derivative is a rate of change; an integral accumulates change over
time.
Velocity is the derivative of position, so motion is naturally expressed
in calculus.
Smoothing and filtering are continuous ideas applied to discrete samples.

---

7. OPTIMISATION
Optimisation finds the inputs that minimise or maximise an objective.
Gradient descent follows the slope of a function toward a minimum, and is
the engine of modern learning.
Fitting and control are both optimisation problems in disguise.

---

8. PROBABILITY
Probability measures uncertainty numerically, between 0 and 1.
A random variable takes values with a distribution; expectation is the
average and variance is the spread.
Measurements are noisy, so quantities are best treated as distributions.

---

9. CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY AND BAYES
Conditional probability is the chance of an event given that another
occurred.
Bayes' rule updates a belief in light of new evidence, which is the
formal basis of filtering and of reasoning about detectors.
It is why a rare event still warrants caution after a positive test.

---

10. ESTIMATION
Estimation infers hidden quantities from noisy measurements.
Estimators trade bias against variance, and accuracy against simplicity.
Tracking and filtering are estimation problems over time.

---

11. LINEAR ALGEBRA IN PRACTICE
Systems of equations, least squares, and eigen-decomposition recur in
fitting, geometry, and stability analysis.
The practical skill is choosing the right representation, not hand
computing large matrices.

---

12. DISCRETE VERSUS CONTINUOUS
Signals, images, and time are sampled, so continuous mathematics is
applied at discrete points.
Sampling has its own rules, covered in the signal-processing document.
Mixing the continuous idea with the discrete implementation causes subtle
errors.

---

13. SUMMARY
Linear algebra represents geometry and transforms; calculus describes
change; optimisation finds the best inputs; probability handles
uncertainty.
These tools are the common language of perception, estimation, control,
and learning.
Comfort with the ideas matters more than formal derivation.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
Perception, control, and learning are the same mathematics wearing
different hats: linear algebra for geometry, calculus for change,
optimisation for choice, and probability for uncertainty. Learn the
ideas, respect finite precision, and always ask which coordinate frame
you are working in.
