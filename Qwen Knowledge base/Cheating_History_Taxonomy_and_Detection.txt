KNOWLEDGE BASE: CHEATING IN GAMES - HISTORY, TAXONOMY, AND DETECTION

Purpose:
Educational reference for understanding cheating in multiplayer games as a
domain: what "cheating" means, how cheating has evolved historically, the
categories of cheats and how each category works conceptually, and how each
category is detected.

This document is reference knowledge. It describes mechanisms and their
consequences at a conceptual level so that they can be reasoned about and
defended against. It is written from a defensive and engineering
perspective and deliberately does not provide recipes for defeating any
specific commercial anti-cheat system.

Related documents: ANTI-CHEAT ARCHITECTURE, ADVERSARIAL AI AND CHEAT
DETECTION, ADAPTIVE CHEATING AND EVOLVING SYSTEMS, INPUT INTEGRITY AND
DEVICE FRAUD, MATCHMAKING AND ACCOUNT ECONOMY INTEGRITY, CHEAT ECONOMICS
AND THE UNDERGROUND MARKET, CLIENT INTEGRITY, ANTI-TAMPER, AND
ATTESTATION, and the numbered technical series.

---

1. WHAT "CHEATING" MEANS
"Cheating" is acting outside the intended rules of play to gain an advantage
the game never meant to grant, or that the other players did not agree to.
It is defined relative to the game's rules and the players' expectations,
not relative to any single technology.
Useful neighbouring terms:
* cheat - alters capability or information beyond the rules.
* exploit - misuses a bug or edge case the rules did not anticipate.
* automation - plays without full human control.
* collusion - breaks the competitive structure between players.
* terms-of-service violation - breaks the operator's contract, which may or
  may not also be a competitive advantage.
Keeping these distinctions is worthwhile, because each has different
detection signals and different remedies.

---

2. WHY PEOPLE CHEAT
Motivation shapes behavior, and behavior shapes detection.
Common motivations:
* competitive advantage - winning, rank, status.
* monetization - boosting, account selling, cheat licensing.
* curiosity and research - understanding how a system works.
* griefing - disrupting other players' experience.
* accessibility and assistance - compensating for ability or hardware.
* social pressure - belonging to a group or community.
A defensive system benefits from understanding motivation, because it partly
predicts which categories of cheat, and which evasions, will follow.

---

3. THE OLDEST CHEAT IS INFORMATION
Before software, cheating meant seeing or doing what others could not:
reading another player's hand, listening to the room, marking cards.
The digital equivalent preserves this core idea.
The most valuable cheat has always been an unfair INFORMATION asymmetry:
knowing something the other player cannot.
This is why the categories below are organised as much around information
channels as around techniques.

---

4. A SHORT HISTORY - ARCADES AND EARLY MACHINES
The earliest video-game cheating targeted high scores and local machines.
Because many arcade and early console games stored scores in memory with no
integrity checking, a player could alter the recorded value directly, or
manipulate the hardware.
There was usually no network and no persistent identity, so "detection"
barely existed; there was only a local record nobody else verified.
This established a lasting lesson: a value stored where its owner can reach
it, and confirmed nowhere else, is not trustworthy.

---

5. A SHORT HISTORY - THE ONLINE FPS ERA (LATE 1990S - 2000S)
Online multiplayer created both the incentive and the audience for software
cheats.
In first-person shooters this produced the archetypes still used today:
* the aimbot - software that assists or takes over aiming.
* the wallhack - software that reveals players through geometry.
* the triggerbot - software that acts automatically when a target is under
  the crosshair.
Cheats read or reconstructed hidden game state and fed it to the player.
Because the client ran on the player's own machine, the player controlled
the very environment being measured - the problem that is still central.
Early client-side scanners and server plugins responded, beginning the arms
race that continues today.

---

6. A SHORT HISTORY - THE MODERN ERA
Later phases added:
* kernel-mode protection - anti-cheat drivers running at the same privilege
  level as anything that could otherwise hide from a user-mode check.
* hardware and external methods - microcontrollers, second machines, and
  DMA-style reads that sit outside the game process entirely.
* visual and machine-learning methods - recovering information from pixels
  rather than from process memory.
* cheating as a service - subscription products, resellers, and frequent
  updates, making cheating an economy rather than a hobby.
Each phase moved either the observation point or the trust boundary, and
each forced a matching shift in defense.

---

7. THE ETERNAL ARMS RACE
Cheats and defenses co-evolve:
CHEAT -> DETECTION -> CHEAT ADAPTATION -> DETECTION ADAPTATION -> ...
Two consequences follow.
First, a detector tuned only to yesterday's cheat becomes obsolete.
Second, the useful research object is not one cheat but the relationship
between a defense's assumptions and the ways those assumptions can fail.

---

8. TWO AXES FOR CLASSIFYING CHEATS
Every cheat can be located on two independent questions.
WHERE IT RUNS relative to the game:
* internal - inside the game process.
* external - a separate process on the same machine.
* external-hardware - another device or machine.
* server-side / network - manipulating the data path.
WHERE IT GETS INFORMATION:
* game memory
* network data
* rendered pixels
* user input
* hardware / side channels
These axes are independent.
A wallhack may be internal or external; an aimbot may be memory-based,
pixel-based, or hardware-based.
The axes matter because the channel determines what is observable, and thus
which defenses are even applicable.

---

9. THE INFORMATION CHANNEL MODEL
Every cheat has an information channel and an action channel.
Example channels:
game memory | network data | rendered pixels | user input | hardware
The question "what can the attacker observe, and what can the defender
observe?" frames most detection problems.
Where the defender controls the information (for example authoritative
server state), detection is stronger; where the attacker controls it (for
example their own machine), detection is weaker.

---

10. CATEGORY - MEMORY READING (INFORMATION CHEATS)
Mechanism, conceptually: the cheat reads the game's own process memory to
recover hidden state - positions, health, visibility, entity lists - that
the game already knows but does not display.
This is the direct, structured-state route: no perception model is needed,
only reading and rendering.
Typical forms: wallhack / ESP overlays, radar, information overlays.
Because it produces structured state rather than pixels, it is a
fundamentally different channel from a visual cheat.
Detection: it requires cross-process access, so it leaves traces a defender
can look for - unexpected handles to the game process, foreign modules,
reads that do not correspond to legitimate callers, and integrity anomalies.
It is countered in part by protected memory, integrity checks, and
kernel-level observation.

---

11. CATEGORY - MEMORY WRITING AND PATCHING (CAPABILITY CHEATS)
Mechanism: instead of reading state, the cheat modifies it - writing values
or patching code so the game behaves differently (for example removing
recoil, spread, or cooldowns, or changing damage).
It changes what the game does, not merely what the player knows.
Detection: code patches and value edits tend to be detectable by
self-integrity checks - comparing running code and critical values against
expected values - and by server-side validation that recomputes outcomes
authoritatively instead of trusting the client.

---

12. CATEGORY - CODE INJECTION AND HOOKING
Mechanism: to read or write another process's state, a cheat must run code
in or around that process, or intercept its calls. Conceptually this is
"load code where the game's code runs, or intercept the calls the game
makes".
Loading a library into a process, mapping code manually, and intercepting
functions are all forms of this.
Because the defender and attacker share the same machine and privilege
level, this is where user-mode versus kernel-mode trust matters most.
Detection: module lists, code-page integrity, API-call anomalies, and
kernel-mode monitoring of the loading and hooking primitives.

---

13. CATEGORY - AIMBOTS AND TRIGGERBOTS
An aimbot is not inherently "artificial intelligence"; mechanically it is a
control system.
Reduced to parts:
OBSERVE target position -> COMPUTE angular or positional error -> APPLY a
correction through a control loop.
If the target position arrives as structured state (memory or network), no
perception is required - only geometry and control. A perception model
exists only to recover the target from pixels when structured state is
unavailable or protected.
Smoothing and humanisation are control-loop shaping, intended to make the
resulting motion resemble human input.
A triggerbot is simpler: it acts when a target condition is satisfied,
adding timing rather than aiming.
Detection: aim behavior is a statistical signal - aim trajectories,
reaction-time distributions, correction patterns, accuracy under conditions
humans find difficult, and consistency across time.

---

14. CATEGORY - AUTOMATION, MACROS, AND INPUT SCRIPTS
Mechanism: instead of reading game state, the cheat replays or generates
INPUT - scripted movement, recoil compensation, repeated actions, or
"one-button" macros.
It may run in software on the machine or in external hardware that presents
itself as an ordinary input device.
This is the boundary case between "assist" and "cheat", and is often defined
by rules rather than by technique.
Detection: input telemetry - inter-event timing that is more regular than
is humanly possible, impossible precision, repeated identical patterns, and
distributions that separate from the human population.

---

15. CATEGORY - HARDWARE AND EXTERNAL-DEVICE CHEATS
Mechanism: move the cheating logic off the monitored machine or into a
device the game cannot inspect - a microcontroller that emulates a mouse, a
small board inserted between mouse and PC, or a second computer that reads
game state another way.
Because the game runs on the machine the player controls, "the machine" is
not a trustworthy witness; hardware moves the attacker outside that
witness's reach.
Detection: this is largely a policy, physical, and platform-trust problem
rather than a pure software scan. Relevant defenses include input-device
attestation, platform integrity, and rules that shift the evidence to
server-side behavioral analysis.

---

16. CATEGORY - NETWORK AND PROTOCOL CHEATS
Mechanism: manipulate the data path rather than the machine - delay or
selectively drop packets ("lag switching"), tamper with protocol messages,
replay or forge actions, or exploit trust the server places in the client.
It also includes exploiting desynchronization between the client's view and
the server's truth.
Detection: server authority is the main defense - validate what the client
claims by recomputing it from authoritative state, reject impossible
transitions, and treat the client as untrusted input. Network statistics
(timing, loss, asymmetry) can also flag manipulation.

---

17. CATEGORY - GAME EXPLOITS (BUGS, NOT SOFTWARE)
Not all unfair advantage comes from external software.
Glitches, geometry exploits, duplication bugs, and unintended mechanics can
be exploited using only the shipped game.
They are cheats in effect, though not in mechanism.
Detection: because no client integrity is broken, these are usually found
by telemetry and by the developers themselves, then fixed in the game, or
answered by rules and moderation.
The long-term remedy is correctness: remove the exploit, do not scan for
it.

---

18. CATEGORY - SOCIAL AND META CHEATS
Some advantages come from manipulating people rather than code:
* collusion / teaming in solo modes.
* boosting and win-trading.
* botting and account farming.
* account sharing and smurfing (deliberately playing below one's rank).
Detection: account and device graphs - relationships between accounts,
shared hardware, behavioral similarity, and population-level statistics.
This reframes the question from "is this executable suspicious?" to "is
this account's behavior and network consistent?"

---

19. CATEGORY - VISUAL AND AI CHEATS
Mechanism: obtain only rendered pixels and recover game state from imagery
with computer vision.
This is the harder route, not the easier one: it throws away structured
state and must reconstruct it.
Its significance is defensive: such a cheat may modify no process and read
no protected memory, so assumptions that "cheating means touching the game"
no longer hold.
Detection: because the software is external and read-only, detection leans
on behavioral analysis and on removing the observation channel (for example
capture protection), rather than on process integrity.

---

20. TAXONOMY SUMMARY
The categories, by information channel:
memory read -> information cheats (wallhack, radar)
memory write -> capability cheats (patch, value edit)
code injection -> enables the above inside the process
pixels -> visual / AI cheats
input -> automation, macros, hardware input
network -> protocol, desync, and trust cheats
people -> social and meta cheats
rules / bugs -> exploits
Each row has a characteristic family of defenses, treated next.

---

21. THE DETECTION PROBLEM
The defender must reason about a machine controlled by the person being
measured.
This asymmetry, not any particular cheat, is the root difficulty.
Progress comes from three directions:
* make some attack paths unavailable (prevent).
* gather independent evidence (detect).
* rely on authority the client cannot fake (server validation, hardware
  trust).

---

22. MATCHING DEFENSES TO CHANNELS
The practical rule: detect a cheat through the channel it uses.
memory / process cheats -> integrity checks, module and handle inspection,
kernel monitoring.
network cheats -> server authority and validation.
input automation -> input telemetry and behavioral statistics.
pixel cheats -> behavioral analysis and control of the observation channel.
hardware -> platform trust, attestation, and policy.
A single defense rarely covers all channels; layering is the norm.

---

23. SIGNATURE DETECTION
The oldest method: recognise a known artifact - a file hash, a byte string,
a named module, or a known tool's fingerprint.
Strengths: cheap, precise, and low false-positive for whatever it matches.
Limits: defeated by any change to the artifact.
Signatures can only catch the past, so they must be combined with behavior
and integrity to stay useful.

---

24. INTEGRITY AND ANTI-TAMPER
Method: verify that the running code and critical values are what they
should be.
Conceptually this includes checking code pages against expected values,
detecting patches, and detecting code that is inspecting the checker.
It raises the cost of tampering and underpins memory and injection
defenses.
Its weakness is the same as the system's: a verifier running on the
attacker's machine can itself be tampered with, which is why verifiers are
moved into protected or kernel-level components.

---

25. BEHAVIOR AND STATISTICAL DETECTION
Method: model what legitimate play looks like, then flag departures.
Common signals:
aim paths and their smoothness or entropy
reaction-time distributions
accuracy under difficult conditions
consistency and repetition
timing regularity of inputs
This is channel-agnostic, so it can catch cheats whose software footprint
is invisible. But it is statistical: it trades false positives against
detection rate, and it drifts as the population changes.

---

26. SERVER-SIDE AUTHORITY AND VALIDATION
Method: do not trust the client's claims; recompute outcomes from
authoritative server state.
The server validates movement, line of sight, damage, and actions, and
rejects impossible transitions.
This is the strongest defense for network, memory-write, and capability
cheats, because the client cannot fabricate a truth the server never
accepted.
Its costs are bandwidth, latency, and design complexity, and some
information (for example precise aim) is genuinely client-side.

---

27. HARDWARE AND PLATFORM TRUST
Method: make the machine's trust state independently verifiable.
Conceptually this includes secure boot, measured boot, TPM-based
attestation, IOMMU / DMA protection, and signed drivers.
The goal is to detect or prevent conditions under which the attacker can
observe or alter the game from outside the software stack.
It is a platform problem, not a game problem, which is why it appears in
anti-cheat but is owned by operating systems and hardware vendors.

---

28. MACHINE-LEARNING DETECTION
Method: learn a classifier of behavior instead of hand-writing rules.
Strength: it finds patterns too complex to specify by hand, and it can
adapt to new variants when retrained.
Costs: it needs labelled data, it can be attacked adversarially, it drifts
as the population changes, and it must be governed (versioned, measured,
calibrated, and rollback-capable).
See ADVERSARIAL AI AND CHEAT DETECTION for depth.

---

29. SIGNALS SUMMARY
A compact mapping of cheat category to primary detection signal:
memory read -> process access and integrity anomalies
memory write -> self-integrity and server recomputation
injection -> module / code-page integrity, kernel monitoring
aimbot -> aim-behavior statistics
automation -> input-timing statistics
hardware -> platform attestation and policy
network -> server validation and network statistics
exploit -> game telemetry and developer fixes
social -> account and device graphs
The strongest systems combine several INDEPENDENT rows.
Repeating the same evidence three times is weaker than three different
kinds of evidence.

---

30. ENFORCEMENT AND INVESTIGATION
Detection produces evidence; enforcement acts on it.
A mature pipeline separates:
DETECT -> CORRELATE -> INVESTIGATE -> DECIDE -> ENFORCE -> APPEAL
Enforcement varies in cost and reversibility: a warning, match
invalidation, a temporary suspension, a permanent ban, and hardware or
account bans.
Because false positives are expensive (they punish legitimate players),
high-confidence, explainable evidence is more valuable than a marginally
higher detection rate.

---

31. FALSE POSITIVES AND COST ASYMMETRY
Two errors exist: missing a cheat (a false negative) and punishing a
legitimate player (a false positive).
Their costs are not symmetric - a false positive damages trust, community,
and business - so thresholds are often set conservatively and combined with
confirmation before enforcement.
This is why "detected" and "banned" are deliberately separate stages.

---

32. THE ADAPTATION LOOP
A detector changes attacker behavior; attacker behavior changes the
population; the changed population changes the detector's data.
Therefore detection is not a static gate but a control loop that must be
monitored, evaluated, and retuned over time.
Static-signature thinking fails here; population-level thinking is
required.

---

33. DETECTING CHEATS VERSUS DETECTING ELITE PLAYERS
The hardest cases are where cheating and legitimately extreme skill look
alike.
Confounds include professional-level mechanics, unusual hardware, and
accessibility tools.
Good systems therefore reason about evidence and uncertainty rather than
asserting certainty from a single signal, and they keep humans in the loop
for ambiguous cases.

---

34. SUMMARY
Cheating is best understood as the pursuit of an unfair information or
capability advantage through a specific channel.
Its history is a sequence of shifting observation points and trust
boundaries.
Its taxonomy is organised by where a cheat runs and which channel it uses.
Its detection is organised by those same channels, layered, and weighed
against the cost of being wrong.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
Every cheat has a channel, and every channel has a defense. Reason about
cheating by asking where it runs and what it can observe, not by the name
of the tool. Detection is not a blacklist but a layered, adaptive control
loop across independent signals; and because the defender measures a
machine the attacker controls, the strongest defenses are the ones that
lean on authority the attacker cannot fake - server-side validation and
hardware/platform trust - rather than on the client's own honesty.
