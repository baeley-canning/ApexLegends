KNOWLEDGE BASE: MATCHMAKING AND ACCOUNT ECONOMY INTEGRITY

Purpose:
Explain the forms of unfairness that are not cheating in the software
sense - smurfing, boosting, win trading, account trading, and bot farms -
and how they undermine matchmaking. It complements CHEATING IN GAMES -
HISTORY, TAXONOMY, AND DETECTION and BAN GOVERNANCE AND IDENTITY
INTEGRITY.

This document is reference knowledge. It describes these behaviours and
the defences against them from a defensive, design perspective. It
contains no instructions for carrying them out.

Practices change quickly and are only partly public, so treat each
statement as a documented CLAIM. Details are given "as of October 2026".

Related documents: BAN GOVERNANCE AND IDENTITY INTEGRITY, CHEATING IN
GAMES - HISTORY, TAXONOMY, AND DETECTION, SERVER-AUTHORITATIVE
ARCHITECTURE AND TRUST BOUNDARIES, and the numbered technical series.

---

1. INTEGRITY BEYOND DETECTION
Not every fairness problem is a cheat running on a machine.
Some are ordinary accounts used dishonestly.
These problems are economic and social, not technical, yet they damage
the same thing anti-cheat protects.
They attack a trustworthy match without any cheat software being
involved.

---

2. THE MATCHMAKING PROMISE
Matchmaking promises that players of similar skill meet.
Every integrity problem below breaks that promise in some way.
Skill rating is the currency, and the misdeeds are attempts to fake or
trade it.
Protecting matchmaking means protecting the meaning of that rating.

---

3. SMURFING
A smurf is a high-skill player on a new, low-rated account.
It is used to farm easy games or to play without consequence.
It is not cheating, but it corrupts the rating of everyone it meets.
Detection leans on statistical patterns: unusually fast climbs, and
skill that does not match the account's history.

---

4. BOOSTING AND RANK MANIPULATION
Boosting is paying someone else to raise an account's rank.
It can be done by playing on the account or by partying with it.
It directly forges the skill signal matchmaking relies on.
Defences include detecting sudden skill discontinuities and linked-account
clusters.

---

5. WIN TRADING AND MATCH FIXING
Win trading is colluding to decide outcomes, so both sides gain rating.
It is common in small high-rank pools where the same players meet
repeatedly.
It is closer to fraud than to cheating, and in organised settings to
match fixing.
Detection looks at outcome patterns that are improbable under fair play.

---

6. ACCOUNT TRADING AND THE BLACK MARKET
Accounts with desirable ranks, items, or history are bought and sold.
Trading moves a rating to a new owner without a new climb.
It also concentrates value in a market that rewards theft and fraud.
Defences include linking accounts to durable identity and detecting
ownership changes through behavioural discontinuities.

---

7. THROWAWAY ACCOUNTS AND BOT FARMS
Cheap accounts make bans affordable to absorb.
If a new account is free and instant, a ban costs little.
Bot farms create such accounts at scale to feed cheating and trading.
This is why account-creation friction is itself an anti-cheat tool.

---

8. TRUST SCORES AND REPUTATION SYSTEMS
Some platforms attach a trust or reputation score to an account or
device.
The score aggregates signals: age, verification, behaviour, and history.
Matchmaking can then separate trusted players from untrusted ones.
This shifts the problem from "is this person cheating?" to "how much do
we trust this account?".

---

9. PHONE AND IDENTITY VERIFICATION
Requiring a verified phone number or identity raises the cost of a new
account.
It links accounts to a scarce resource and slows ban evasion.
It also raises privacy and access concerns, especially for players
without a number or in restrictive regions.
The trade-off is friction for abusers against friction for everyone
else.

---

10. WHY BANS DO NOT STOP IT
A ban addresses an account, but the economy rewards new accounts.
Without identity friction, banning is a cost of doing business.
This is why enforcement alone cannot fix matchmaking integrity.
The problem is structural, so the fix must be structural too.

---

11. DESIGN LEVERS
Several levers raise the cost of abuse without punishing honest players:
account-creation friction, verification, trust-based matchmaking, rating
that resists fast inflation, and detection of linked clusters.
Each lever trades some convenience for some integrity.
The right mix depends on the game and its community.

---

12. THE TENSION WITH HONEST PLAYERS
Every anti-abuse measure also burdens legitimate newcomers.
Too much friction and the game cannot grow; too little and integrity
collapses.
Getting this balance right is a product decision as much as a technical
one.
It is the same proportionality problem that runs through all anti-abuse
design.

---

13. SUMMARY
Matchmaking integrity covers dishonesty that is not cheat software:
smurfing, boosting, win trading, account trading, and bot farms.
All of them forge or trade the skill signal that matchmaking depends on.
Bans alone cannot fix it because accounts are cheap to replace.
The structural defences are account friction, verification, trust scores,
and rating that resists manipulation.
The governing tension is always friction for abusers against friction for
honest players.

CORE PRINCIPLE:
Matchmaking integrity is an economic problem wearing a technical mask. The
skill rating is the asset, and the misdeeds are ways to fake or trade it,
so the strongest defences make a dishonest account expensive to create and
hard to hand over. Detect the patterns, but fix the incentives.
